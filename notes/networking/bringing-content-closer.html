One reliable way to make a service feel faster to people around the world is to put copies of its content on machines spread out near where those people are, instead of serving everything from one distant place. There are two reasons this helps, one obvious and one that is easy to miss.

The obvious reason: distance

Data cannot travel faster than physics allows. A machine near the user simply answers sooner than one on the other side of the planet, because the signal has less distance to cover. Shorter distance also softens the startup penalties that network connections pay when they first get going, so the whole exchange feels snappier. Closer is faster, plainly.

The subtle reason: routing

The less obvious reason is about how traffic finds its way across the internet. The public internet does not necessarily send your data along the fastest route. Its routing decisions are based on coarse properties, not on which path is actually quickest or least congested for your particular exchange. So the path you get may be far from the best one.

A network of well-placed machines with their own tuned links between them can do better. It can carry your data over paths it controls and has optimized, rather than leaving it to the open internet's rougher choices. In other words, part of the benefit is not storing content nearby at all, but moving it along a better network than you would otherwise get.

Why it helps even without caching

This is why such a network helps even for content that changes constantly and cannot be stored ahead of time. Even when it has nothing cached to hand you, it still acts as a fast, well-connected front door: it takes your request in nearby, carries it efficiently back to the origin, and returns the answer over its better paths. As a bonus, sitting in front like this lets it absorb floods of hostile traffic before they reach your real servers.

The balance in how many places

There is a trade-off in how many locations to run. More locations mean each one is closer to someone, which is good. But each location also sees a smaller slice of the total demand, so any given item is less likely to already be sitting there when asked for. Spreading out improves distance but hurts the odds of a ready copy. The usual answer is layers: many small locations near users for the popular content, backed by fewer, larger ones that hold the long tail, so you stay close without missing too often.

The point

Putting content near people speeds things up for two reasons: less distance to travel, and better routing than the open internet provides on its own. Because the routing part does not depend on having a stored copy, such a network helps even for uncacheable content and can shield your servers from attacks. Just mind the balance: more locations bring content closer but make each one less likely to have what is asked for, which is why layering pays off.

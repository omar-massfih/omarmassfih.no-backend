When you send data across a network, it passes through a series of routers, each handing it one step closer to its destination. Here is the puzzle: no single router knows the whole network. Yet each one somehow picks the right next step. They pull this off by cooperating, and there are two classic ways to do it.

Share a full map

In the first approach, every router shares its own small piece of local knowledge: who it is directly connected to, and how costly each of those links is. Every router announces just its own connections to all the others. Once everyone has heard everyone else's local pieces, each router can assemble the same complete map of the network and work out the best path to anywhere by itself.

This is robust and gives everyone a consistent picture. Its cost is weight: every router has to receive and process updates from every other one, which grows expensive as the network gets large.

Share only distances

In the second approach, routers do not build a full map. Each one only tells its immediate neighbors how far it thinks each destination is. To update itself, a router takes a neighbor's claimed distance to some place and adds its own cost to reach that neighbor, then keeps whichever total is best.

This is lighter: you only talk to your neighbors, and you only exchange short summaries rather than whole maps. Good news, like a new shorter path appearing, spreads quickly from neighbor to neighbor.

The trap in trusting summaries

Sharing only distances has a famous flaw. After a link fails, routers can start echoing stale distances back and forth. A router hears a neighbor claim it still has a path to some destination, not realizing that the neighbor's path actually ran back through the router itself, along the link that just died. So it trusts a route that secretly depended on it, the numbers creep upward step by step, and it can take a long time for everyone to realize the destination is really gone.

The deeper point generalizes well beyond networks. Sharing full information gives everyone an accurate, consistent view at a high cost. Sharing only summaries is cheap, but you act on second-hand claims whose hidden dependencies you cannot see, and those hidden dependencies can quietly loop back on you.

The takeaway

Routing is a distributed agreement about paths, assembled from purely local knowledge. You can have every router broadcast its local links so all of them build the same full map, accurate but expensive, or have each router share only its distance estimates with neighbors, cheap but capable of being misled by stale claims that secretly depended on the listener. It is the same trade whenever independent parties pool knowledge: the whole picture costs more but lies less.

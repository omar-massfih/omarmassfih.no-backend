A network is shared. Many connections send data through the same links and the same junction points in the middle. Each sender would love to go as fast as possible. The problem is what happens when they all do at once: the shared middle cannot keep up, and everyone loses.

The traffic jam

Picture a junction where data piles up faster than it can be passed along. A queue forms. If the queue keeps growing, either delays become enormous, or the junction runs out of room and starts throwing data away. Congestion is just this: more traffic offered than some part of the network can handle.

Now it gets worse. When data is dropped, senders notice it did not arrive and resend it. So exactly when the network is overloaded, everyone adds even more traffic by resending. That extra load causes more drops, which causes more resends. Without something to stop it, the whole network can spiral down into doing almost no useful work, a jam that feeds on itself.

Guessing a safe speed

No sender can see the whole network or know how much room is left, so it cannot simply be told the right speed. Instead each sender feels its way to a safe rate using a strikingly simple rule: when things are going well, creep a little faster; the moment there is a sign of trouble, back off sharply.

The sign of trouble is usually a lost piece of data, since loss often means a queue somewhere overflowed. So a sender nudges its rate up bit by bit while data flows cleanly, and cuts it hard, often to something like half, as soon as it sees a loss. Gentle up, sharp down. This keeps each sender probing for a little more speed while pulling back quickly whenever it has pushed too far.

Why this shares the network fairly

That "gentle up, sharp down" rhythm has a lovely side effect: it shares a busy link fairly. A sender that is hogging more than its share is, by definition, sending a lot, so when congestion causes a loss, its sharp cut takes away a big chunk. A sender using less gives up less. Over time the greedy ones are pulled down harder and the modest ones catch up, and they settle toward roughly equal shares without anyone coordinating.

Starting out carefully

There is also the question of how to begin, when a sender has no idea what the network can take. Rather than guess high and cause an instant jam, it starts small and ramps up quickly, roughly doubling its rate each round until it sees the first sign of a limit. It begins cautious, finds the ceiling fast, and then settles into the gentle-up, sharp-down rhythm around it.

Worth remembering

If every sender sent as fast as it could, the shared middle would collapse into a self-worsening jam. The fix is for each sender to hold back on its own: speed up slowly while things are fine, slow down sharply at the first sign of congestion, and start gently when it knows nothing. Out of that local, selfish caution comes a network that stays stable and, remarkably, divides itself fairly.

The internet, underneath, makes no promises. Pieces of your data can be lost, can arrive twice, or can show up out of order. Yet when you load a page or send a file, it arrives whole and in the right order. That reliability is not in the network itself; it is built on top of an unreliable network by the two ends working together.

Number everything, then confirm it

The foundation is simple. The sender numbers every piece of data it sends. The receiver, as pieces arrive, tells the sender how much it has received in order so far, essentially saying "I have everything up to here." With numbering and these confirmations, both sides can spot trouble: a gap in the numbers means something is missing, a repeat means a duplicate, and out-of-order pieces can be put back in order using their numbers.

When a piece is not confirmed within a reasonable time, the sender assumes it was lost and sends it again. That is the whole reliability mechanism: keep track of what has been confirmed, and resend what has not.

The trouble with "a reasonable time"

How long should the sender wait before deciding a piece was lost? This is a genuine trade-off. Wait too short a time and it will resend things that were actually fine but just slow, wasting effort and adding load. Wait too long and it is slow to recover when something really was lost, leaving the connection stalled.

Since network delays vary, the sender cannot use a fixed guess. Instead it watches how long confirmations are actually taking and adjusts its waiting time to match, smoothing over the natural bumpiness so a single slow moment does not trigger a flood of needless resends.

Do not wait for each confirmation

Sending one piece, waiting for its confirmation, then sending the next would be dreadfully slow, because the sender spends most of its time idle waiting for the round trip. So instead it keeps several pieces in flight at once, sending more before earlier confirmations arrive. It keeps a copy of anything not yet confirmed, in case it needs to resend. This keeps the connection busy instead of stalling after every single piece.

Do not drown a slow receiver

There is one more danger: the sender might be faster than the receiver can keep up with. If it blasts data faster than the receiver can process it, the extra just overflows and is lost. To prevent this, the receiver tells the sender how much room it currently has, and the sender never sends more than that. As the receiver works through its backlog and frees up room, it raises the amount. If it is completely full, it says so, and the sender pauses until space opens up. The receiver, in effect, controls the tap.

The point

Reliable delivery is not something the network provides; it is something the two endpoints construct. Number the pieces, confirm what arrived, resend what did not, and tune the waiting time to real conditions. Keep several pieces in flight so the link stays busy, but never send faster than the receiver says it can handle. Out of those simple agreements comes a clean, ordered stream over a network that guarantees none of it.

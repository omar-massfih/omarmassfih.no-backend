When a serious outage hits, the hard part is often not the technical fix. It is the coordination: several people, some of whom do not normally work together, trying to understand the problem, try things, and keep everyone informed all at once. Without structure, that turns into a scramble where work is duplicated, decisions stall, and nobody knows who is doing what.

Give people clear roles

The fix is to hand out clear roles at the start. One person coordinates: they hold the overall picture, decide what happens next, and pull in help, but they do not get sucked into hands-on debugging themselves, because someone has to keep the big picture. Others do the actual investigating and fixing. And someone owns communication, keeping everyone outside the response updated.

Separating these matters because they conflict under pressure. The person deep in the logs cannot also be tracking who to notify and what to try next. When one person tries to do everything, things get dropped. Distinct roles mean each important job has someone actually responsible for it.

Communicate even when there is nothing new

People outside the response are anxious and in the dark. If they hear nothing, they assume the worst: that nothing is being done, or that a long-fixed problem is still going. So send regular updates, even ones that just say "still working on it, next update in thirty minutes." Silence gets filled with bad guesses. Steady updates keep everyone calm and stop a flood of "is it fixed yet?" questions from pulling responders away from the work.

Stop the bleeding first

In the moment, making the pain stop matters more than fully understanding the cause. If a broad action like undoing the last change makes the problem go away, that is often worth doing before you know exactly what went wrong. These blunt fixes are not precise and can cause their own disruption, so they are a trade-off, but getting users working again buys you calm time to find the real cause without the clock screaming at you.

Practice before it is real

You cannot learn coordination for the first time during a real emergency, when stress is highest. Teams that handle incidents well have practiced: running drills, or treating a minor problem as a formal incident just to rehearse the roles and the communication. That practice builds habits, so when the real one comes, people fall into their roles instead of freezing. The structure matters more the bigger the incident gets, because that is when the most unfamiliar people are suddenly working together.

Worth remembering

A big outage is a coordination problem as much as a technical one. Assign clear roles so someone owns coordinating, someone owns fixing, and someone owns communicating. Send updates even when there is no news, reach first for whatever stops the immediate pain, and practice the whole dance before you need it. Calm under pressure is not a personality trait; it is the result of having a structure to fall back on.

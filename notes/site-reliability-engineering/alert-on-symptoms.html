Monitoring is how a team watches a running system: collecting numbers about what it is doing and turning them into something people can see. The hard part is not collecting data. It is deciding when that data should wake a human up in the middle of the night.

Symptoms and causes

A symptom is something a user feels: the page will not load, requests are failing, answers come back slowly. A cause is a reason behind it: a full disk, a crashed process, a slow database. The difference matters because they answer different questions. A symptom tells you what is broken for users. A cause suggests why.

The best alerts are based on symptoms. A full disk is only worth waking someone for if it is actually hurting users. Sometimes a disk fills up and nothing breaks, because the system routed around it. If you alert on the cause, you page a person for a problem that may not be a problem. If you alert on the symptom, you only interrupt someone when users are genuinely affected.

Two ways to watch a system

One way is to test the system from the outside, the way a user would: send a request, see if it works. This catches real, user-visible failures as they happen, which makes it a good basis for paging someone.

The other way is to look inside the system at its internal numbers: queue lengths, error counts, memory use. This is less useful for paging, because an internal warning may never reach users, but it is invaluable for debugging and for spotting trouble before it becomes visible. Both are useful; they just serve different jobs.

What makes an alert worth sending

Every page has a cost: it interrupts a person and pulls their attention. That cost is only worth paying when the alert is urgent, points to a real problem, and needs a human to think. If a page fires often for things that turn out to be fine, people stop trusting it. Then a genuine emergency gets the same tired shrug as the false alarms. This is called alert fatigue, and it is how real incidents get missed.

There is a sharper test. If an alert always has the same fix, and a person just follows the same steps every time, that alert should not be waking anyone. It should be automated. A page that leads to a predictable, rote response is a task a computer should be doing, not a reason to interrupt a human.

Why averages hide problems

When you do measure the system, be careful how you summarize it. An average can look healthy while some users are suffering. If most requests are fast but a small share are very slow, the average stays low and hides the slow ones. Yet those slow requests may be exactly the broken experiences you care about.

It also helps to separate successful and failed requests. Failures are often fast, because giving up quickly is quicker than doing the work. Mixed together, a burst of fast failures can even make the overall numbers look better while users are getting errors. Looking at the slow tail, and at failures on their own, keeps these hidden problems visible.

In short

Page people for what users feel, not for every internal hiccup. Keep the inside view for understanding and prevention. Make sure every alert is worth the interruption, and turn any alert with a fixed, repeatable fix into automation. The goal is simple: when the pager goes off, it should mean something real is wrong and a person is genuinely needed.

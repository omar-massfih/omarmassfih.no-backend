It sounds obvious that a service should be as reliable as possible. In practice, chasing perfect reliability is a mistake. Past a certain point, each extra bit of reliability costs a lot and gives users almost nothing they can feel. The money and engineering time spent removing a rare failure could have gone into new features, lower prices, or paying down old problems.

So the useful question is not "how do we never fail?" It is "how reliable does this service actually need to be?" The answer comes from the users and the business, not from a wish for perfection.

Reliability is a target, not a maximum

A reliability target works in both directions. Falling below it hurts users and should trigger action. But sitting far above it is also a signal, just a different one: it means the team is spending effort on reliability that nobody asked for, effort that could have gone somewhere more useful.

Two costs push back against endless reliability. One is the extra hardware and spare capacity you have to run. The other is the time engineers spend reducing risk instead of building things users want. Both are real, and both grow quickly as you get closer to perfect.

From a target to a budget

A team first agrees on a reliability target: for example, that a service should handle 99.9% of requests successfully over a month. This target is often called a service level objective, or SLO. It is written as a goal the team promises to meet, not a description of how the system happens to behave today.

The gap between perfect and the target is the interesting part. If the goal is 99.9%, then 0.1% of requests are allowed to fail. That small slice of allowed failure is the error budget. It is not a mistake to be ashamed of. It is a resource the team is free to spend.

What the budget is for

The budget solves a very human problem. Teams that build features want to ship fast. Teams that keep the service running want stability. Without a shared number, that tension gets settled by whoever argues hardest, or by fear after the last outage.

An error budget replaces the argument with a rule. As long as the service is inside its budget, new releases can keep going out. When the budget runs low, the same number says the opposite: slow down, stabilize, and stop taking risks until reliability recovers. Now both sides are looking at one agreed measure instead of pushing personal opinions.

An important detail: everything counts against the budget, not just risky releases. A datacenter losing power or a network fault burns budget the same way a bad deploy does. The whole team owns availability, whatever the cause, so the whole team shares the same limited budget.

Why 100% is the wrong goal

Setting the target at 100% quietly does harm. It leaves no room to release changes, since any change carries some risk. It pushes teams to overbuild for failures that almost never happen. And it usually costs far more than users would ever pay for.

It is also easier to start with a loose target and tighten it later than to start strict and loosen it. Loosening a promise after users have relied on it feels like taking something away. Tightening a gentle target as you learn more feels like steady improvement.

The takeaway

Reliability is a trade-off, not a virtue you can max out. Pick a target that matches what users actually need, treat the leftover room as a budget, and let that budget decide when to ship and when to slow down. The point is not to avoid every failure. It is to spend a known, agreed amount of failure on purpose, and put the saved effort where it does more good.

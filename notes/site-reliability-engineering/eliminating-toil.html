Every team that runs software has a pile of repetitive operational work: restarting a stuck job, running the same recovery script, applying the same small fix over and over. This kind of work has a name in reliability engineering. It is called toil, and the reason it has a name is that it is easy to underestimate and easy to let grow.

What makes work toil

Toil is not simply work you dislike. It has a specific shape. It is manual, repetitive, and reactive. It can be automated. And, most importantly, it leaves no lasting improvement: once you finish, the service is in the same state it was before. You did the task, and next week you will do it again.

A useful test is to ask what is different after the work is done. If the answer is "nothing, until it happens again," it is toil. If the answer is "the problem can no longer happen, or now fixes itself," it is engineering.

Running a script by hand still counts as toil, even though a script is doing the steps. The script automates the steps, but a person still has to notice the problem, decide to act, and press the button. The human time is the toil, not the individual commands.

Why toil grows if you let it

Toil expands to fill whatever time it is given. There is always one more manual fix that seems quicker to just do than to automate away. Each small task feels harmless on its own, so the pile grows without anyone deciding to let it.

Worse, some toil grows with the service. If handling twice the traffic or twice the customers means twice the manual work, the team has to grow at the same rate just to keep up. That is the trap a healthy team wants to avoid: a service should be able to grow a lot without the operational work growing just as fast.

Why it matters beyond annoyance

Toil is not only tedious, it is quietly damaging. For a person, a job that is mostly toil leads to boredom, stalled skills, and burnout. For a team, heavy toil crowds out the projects that would have removed the toil in the first place, so the team stays stuck reacting instead of improving.

There is also a slow drift in what the team becomes. A team that spends its days on manual operations starts to look like a team whose only job is manual operations, and it attracts more of that work. Protecting time for real engineering is what keeps a team able to build its way out.

When grungy work is not toil

Not all unglamorous work is toil. Setting up monitoring, tuning a system so it stops failing, or writing the automation that ends a recurring chore can all be dull and fiddly. But they leave a lasting improvement behind, so they are engineering, not toil. The dividing line is enduring value, not how pleasant the task feels.

The bottom line

Treat toil as something to measure and push back on, not as an unavoidable fact of running software. Ask of repetitive work: does this leave anything better behind? If not, the goal is to automate it away or design it out, and to protect enough time to actually do that. Otherwise the manual work quietly wins.

You have just connected to a stranger who claims to be a particular party. How can you tell they really are who they say? They can send you their claimed identity, but anyone can claim any name. A claim by itself proves nothing, and you cannot have personally arranged trust with every stranger in the world ahead of time. That does not scale.

Delegate the trust

The way out is to delegate. You trust the stranger's identity not because you know them, but because someone you already trust vouches for them. The stranger presents more than "I am so-and-so"; they also present a trusted party's endorsement confirming it. If you trust that party, and that party vouches for the stranger, then you can trust the stranger too, without ever having met.

It is the digital version of "any friend of a friend is a friend." You extend your trust along an introduction from someone whose word you already accept.

Why it chains

This delegation links into a chain. The party who vouched for the stranger may in turn be vouched for by an even more established party, and so on up to a small number of well-known authorities at the top. You only need to directly trust those few roots. Everyone else is trusted transitively, through the chain of endorsements leading back to a root you accept.

That is what makes it scale. Instead of pre-arranging trust with millions of strangers, you pre-arrange it with a handful of roots, and they, directly or through others, vouch for the rest.

As strong as its weakest link

Two cautions come with this power. First, the whole structure is only as trustworthy as the roots and every link in the chain. If a trusted voucher is careless or gets compromised, every identity it endorsed inherits the problem, and you would have accepted them all. Delegated trust concentrates risk at the top.

Second, these endorsements usually expire on purpose, so trust has to be renewed periodically. A useful consequence, and an occasional nasty surprise, is that a lapsed endorsement can lock out a perfectly healthy system: others correctly refuse to trust an identity whose vouching has run out, even though nothing else is wrong. The service works fine; it just can no longer prove who it is.

The point

You cannot trust a stranger's claimed identity on its own, and you cannot know every stranger in advance. So you delegate: trust a few authorities, and trust whoever they vouch for, transitively along a chain back to a root you accept. It is a powerful way to scale trust, as strong as its weakest link, and quietly dependent on keeping the vouching current.

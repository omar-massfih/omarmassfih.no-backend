A simple, powerful security idea is to give every person and every program only the access their actual job needs, and nothing more. If a task only reads one kind of record, it should not also be able to delete the whole database. This is least privilege, and its value is easiest to see when something goes wrong.

Why narrow access matters

Whatever access a person or program has is also the access an attacker gains if they take it over, and the damage a careless mistake can cause. Narrow that access and you narrow the damage. A stolen account that can touch one small thing is a small problem; the same account with the keys to everything is a disaster. Least privilege caps how far any single failure, malicious or accidental, can reach.

That reach has a name worth keeping in mind: blast radius. The whole point of least privilege is to keep the blast radius of any one compromise or blunder as small as possible, so a bad day does not become a catastrophe.

Reading can be as dangerous as changing

It is tempting to guard the ability to change or delete data far more carefully than the ability to just read it. But reading sensitive data can be its own disaster. Once secrets or private records have been seen and copied, the harm is done and cannot be undone, even though nothing was modified. Read access to the wrong thing deserves the same respect as write access.

Match the guard to the risk

Not everything needs the same level of protection. Guard everything equally and you either leave the dangerous things underprotected or wrap the harmless things in so much friction that people are miserable. The better approach is to sort access by how much harm it could do, and put the strongest controls where the risk is highest. Ordinary actions stay easy; sensitive ones get extra checks.

One especially useful control is access that expires. Instead of granting powerful permissions forever, grant them for as long as the task needs and let them lapse. Permanent power tends to pile up quietly and be forgotten; expiring access keeps the standing pile of authority small, and creates natural moments to log and review who needed what.

An unusable control is no control

Here is the part that is easy to forget: if asking for access is too painful, people will find a way around it. They will share credentials, keep broad permissions "to save time," or route through some back door. A control so annoying that everyone bypasses it protects nothing. Usability is therefore part of security, not a separate nicety. The safe path has to also be the easy path, or it will not be the path people take.

The upshot

Give each person and program the narrowest access that lets them do their job, treat reading sensitive data as seriously as changing it, and put your strongest controls where the potential harm is greatest. Prefer access that expires over access that lingers. And make the secure way usable, because a control people route around is no protection at all.

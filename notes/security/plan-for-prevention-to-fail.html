A tempting way to think about safety is to build one strong wall and keep every bad thing out. It does not hold up. Perfect prevention is not achievable: eventually a mistake, a bug, or an attacker gets past the wall. The systems that survive are the ones designed on the assumption that prevention will sometimes fail.

Layers, not a single wall

The first idea is to have more than one line of defense. If you rely on a single protection, then the day it fails, everything is exposed. If instead there are several independent protections stacked behind each other, one of them failing is not the end: the next one still stands. This is defense in depth, and its logic is simple. The chance of all layers failing at once is far smaller than the chance of any one failing.

Divide the system into zones

The second idea is to make sure that when something does get through, it cannot spread everywhere. Split the system into separate zones, so a failure, a stolen password, or a compromised machine is trapped inside one zone instead of reaching the whole system. This keeps the blast radius small: the damage is contained to a section rather than sinking everything. The same walls that stop an attacker spreading also stop an ordinary failure cascading.

Simple is safer

The third idea is to keep the design simple. Complexity hides connections you did not know existed, so a small, innocent-looking change can trigger a large failure through some dependency nobody remembered. A simpler system has fewer dark corners, fewer surprising interactions, and is far easier to reason about during an emergency, when you least want to be untangling something clever. Simplicity is not just tidiness; it directly reduces how much can go wrong.

Assume you will need to recover

The last idea follows from the first: since something will eventually get through, you need to be able to notice it and recover, not just prevent it. Build in ways to detect that something is wrong and ways to return to a safe state. And when you push out a change, roll it out gradually rather than to everything at once, so a faulty or malicious change is caught while it has only reached a small slice, instead of after it has hit the entire system.

Putting it together

Do not stake everything on keeping problems out, because sometimes they get in. Stack independent defenses so one failure is not fatal, divide the system into zones so trouble cannot spread, keep the design simple so it holds fewer surprises, and plan to detect and recover rather than only to prevent. Good safety is not one perfect wall; it is a system that stays standing when a wall comes down.

Getting data reliably from one machine to another, every byte arriving in order, sounds like it should be enough. For security, it is not, and the gap is wider than people expect. Plain reliable delivery sends everything in the open and proves nothing about who is on the other end. Making a connection actually secure takes three separate things, and missing any one leaves a hole.

Secrecy

First, the data must be encrypted, so that anyone listening in sees gibberish instead of your content. This is the part everyone thinks of, and it is genuinely necessary: without it, every router and network along the path can read everything you send.

Authentication

Second, you must actually know who you are talking to. This is the one people forget, and it is the sharpest trap. Encrypting a message to a party whose identity you never verified is useless, because you might be encrypting it flawlessly for an impostor sitting in the middle, who then reads it and passes it along.

Secrecy without knowing the recipient is a locked box handed to a stranger you merely assumed was your friend. The encryption worked perfectly; it just protected your secret all the way into the wrong hands. Verifying identity is what makes the encryption mean anything.

Integrity

Third, you must be able to tell whether the data was tampered with along the way. Even if a message is encrypted and headed to the right party, you want to detect if someone altered it in flight. Integrity protection catches meddling, so a change made in transit does not slip through unnoticed.

All three, everywhere

A connection that only encrypts, without confirming identity or detecting tampering, is not secure; it just feels secure, which is more dangerous than knowing you are exposed. You need secrecy, authentication, and integrity together.

One practical note: this is not only for traffic crossing the public internet. Internal networks are not automatically trustworthy either, and securing connections is cheap enough now that it is usually worth doing everywhere, not just at the edge.

Putting it together

Reliably moving bytes is not the same as securing them. A secure connection must keep the data secret, confirm who is on the other end, and prove the data was not altered on the way. And remember the trap at the center of it: encryption without knowing who you are talking to is a comforting illusion, not protection.

Security and reliability sound like the same goal wearing two hats: a good system should be both safe and dependable. Most of the time they do reinforce each other. But now and then they point in opposite directions, and noticing where they split is what keeps you from being blindsided.

The moment of failure

The clearest split appears when something breaks. Suppose the part of your system that checks whether someone is allowed in stops responding. You have two choices, and they are opposites.

You can let people through when the check is down, so real users are not blocked. That keeps the service available, which is what reliability wants. But it may also hand an attacker a way straight past your controls, precisely when you cannot verify who they are.

Or you can shut the door when the check is down, so no one gets in without a proper check. That keeps you protected, which is what security wants. But it can also lock out legitimate users, and sometimes the very responders trying to fix the outage, exactly when access is most urgent.

Neither answer is universally right. Which failure you prefer depends on what you are protecting: staying open by default suits a public reading service, while staying closed by default suits a system holding money or secrets.

Even helpful things cut both ways

The tension shows up elsewhere too. Keeping spare copies and extra paths makes a system more reliable, because users can survive a component failing. But every extra path is also another way in: to stay safe you must defend all of them, while an attacker only has to find one that is weak. The same redundancy that helps reliability quietly enlarges the surface security has to guard.

No attacker required

It also works the other way: a plain accident can be a security failure with nobody attacking at all. A message delivered to the wrong person leaks private data just as surely as a break-in would. Corruption or an outage can break the promises about confidentiality, integrity, and availability that security is supposed to keep. Reliability problems and security problems are not separate bins; one can become the other.

What to remember

Treat security and reliability as usually-allied but sometimes-opposed. When a control fails, decide in advance whether you would rather stay open for real users or stay closed to attackers, because you cannot always have both. Remember that redundancy helps uptime while widening the attack surface, and that an accident can breach security with no adversary present. Seeing these tensions ahead of time lets you choose deliberately, instead of discovering the trade-off mid-incident.

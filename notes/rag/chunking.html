Before text is retrieved, the source document has to be cut into pieces. This is chunking. Chunking decides the unit of retrieval: the smallest piece the system searches for and passes to the model.

Using a whole document as one unit usually works poorly. The embedding becomes too broad, search gets vague, and the model receives more text than it needs. Good chunking gives retrieval smaller, clearer pieces to work with.

Why not whole documents?

Two limits force the split. First, an embedding is one fixed-length vector. The more text you compress into one vector, the more the meaning blurs. A vector for a whole page mixes every topic on the page into one point, so search becomes less precise.

Second, the model has a limited context window, and every token has a cost. You want to send the passages most relevant to the question, not entire files with only a few useful lines inside them.

The size trade-off

Chunk size is the main dial, and it cuts both ways. Small chunks are precise. Each vector captures a narrow idea, so retrieval points closer to the exact passage. But a chunk might be too small to stand on its own if it loses the surrounding context.

Large chunks carry more context, but they also dilute the embedding and spend prompt space on text unrelated to the question. There is no single right size. It depends on the source material and the questions people ask. Dense reference text often works with smaller chunks. Narrative or argument often needs larger chunks so each passage keeps its thread.

Overlap

A hard cut might land in the middle of a thought. It might split a sentence, separate a claim from its support, or leave a reference without the thing it refers to. Overlap softens this by sharing a small amount of text between neighboring chunks.

With overlap, an idea near a boundary still appears whole in at least one chunk. The cost is duplication in the store and a little extra retrieval noise. For many systems, this is a fair price for preserving meaning at the edges.

Split along structure

The blunt approach is to cut every N characters. It is simple, but it ignores the document's own structure. A better default is to split along boundaries the author already provided: headings, sections, paragraphs, and lists.

A section is a unit the writer chose to keep together, so it is often a good retrieval unit too. Splitting along structure keeps related sentences in the same chunk and keeps unrelated ones apart. Use size limits as a fallback when a section is too large.

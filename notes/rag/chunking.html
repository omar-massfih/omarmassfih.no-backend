Before anything can be retrieved, the source has to be cut into pieces. This is chunking. You cannot embed a whole document as one vector and expect useful search, and you cannot feed an entire book into the prompt. Chunking decides the unit of retrieval: the smallest thing the system can find and hand to the model.

Why not whole documents?

Two limits force the split. First, an embedding is a single fixed-size vector, so the more text you compress into it, the more the meaning blurs. A vector for a whole page mixes every topic on that page into one point, and search becomes vague. Second, the model's context window is finite and expensive, so you want to send only the passages that matter, not entire files.

The size trade-off

Chunk size is the main dial, and it cuts both ways. Small chunks are precise: each vector captures one idea, so retrieval can point at the exact passage. But a chunk can be too small to stand on its own, missing the context that makes it meaningful. Large chunks carry more context, but they dilute the embedding and waste prompt space on text that is not relevant to the question.

There is no single right size. It depends on the material and the questions. Dense reference text tolerates smaller chunks; narrative or argument often needs larger ones so a passage keeps its thread.

Overlap

A hard cut can land in the middle of a thought, splitting a sentence or separating a claim from its support. Overlap softens this: consecutive chunks share a little text at their edges, so an idea that straddles a boundary still appears whole in at least one chunk. The cost is some duplication in the store, which is usually a fair price for not losing meaning at the seams.

Split along structure

The blunt approach is to cut every N characters. It is simple, but it ignores what the document is telling you. A better default is to split along the structure the author already provided: headings, sections, paragraphs. A section is a unit the writer decided belongs together, which makes it a natural retrieval unit too. Splitting on structure keeps related sentences in the same chunk and keeps unrelated ones apart, which is exactly what good retrieval needs. Fall back to size limits only to break up a section that is too large.

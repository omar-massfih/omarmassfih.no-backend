For my master's thesis at the University of Oslo I looked at a practical question about retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Students increasingly ask large language models questions about their courses, and a useful answer needs course context that is both correct and relevant. The full course material is usually too large to put in the model's context window, so a RAG system retrieves smaller pieces, or chunks, and adds only those to the prompt. If the retrieved chunks miss the answer, the model is left to guess.

Course material tends to come in two very different forms: lecture slides and textbooks. Slides are short and fragmented. Textbooks are long and written in full prose. My thesis asked whether a dense retriever finds the relevant chunks more effectively in one form than the other, and whether any difference actually holds up or just depends on how the comparison is set up.

How I set it up

I collected slides and textbooks from three university courses: algorithms, software testing, and medical physiology. For each course I built a ground-truth dataset of 70 questions, where every question could be answered from both the slides and the textbook. Then I manually labeled the chunks for each question by whether they contained the answer, on a scale of 0 for no, 1 for somewhat, and 2 for yes.

Retrieval was scored with mean reciprocal rank at ten (MRR@10), which measures how early the first relevant chunk shows up in the top ten results. I compared uniform chunking, where both sources are split the same way, against source-specific chunking, where each source is split according to its own structure. I also added a rewriting condition, rewriting one source into the shape of the other, and a manual retrieval condition, where I handed the correct chunks straight to the model to see how good the answers could get.

What I found

With source-specific chunking, textbooks came out slightly ahead across the courses (mean MRR@10 of 0.984 for textbooks against 0.975 for slides). With uniform chunking the picture was mixed: algorithms favored slides, while software testing and medical physiology favored textbooks (0.952 for textbooks against 0.933 for slides). So the "winner" changed depending on the chunking strategy.

Rewriting one source into the form of the other did not help. It lowered retrieval for both sources, and the effect varied by course (slides went from 0.933 to 0.908, textbooks from 0.952 to 0.946). That suggests the difference between slides and textbooks is not just about surface structure.

The most interesting result came from the manual retrieval condition. When I gave the model the correct chunks directly, slides produced more correct answers than textbooks in all three courses (mean answer correctness of 0.766 for slides against 0.649 for textbooks). In other words, the source that was slightly easier to retrieve from was not the source that produced the best answers once retrieval was taken out of the equation.

What it means

The main takeaway is that comparing source types in RAG is sensitive to how you set up the comparison. Slides and textbooks are not inherently better or worse retrieval sources on their own. The result depends on the chunking strategy and the evaluation setup, so a RAG system for course material should be evaluated on the specific material and setup it is meant to support.

The gap between retrieval effectiveness and answer correctness is the part I keep coming back to. It is easy to optimize a RAG system for retrieval scores and assume better retrieval means better answers. My results show that is not guaranteed. Getting the right chunk in front of the model is only half the problem. What the model does with that chunk is the other half, and the two do not always agree.

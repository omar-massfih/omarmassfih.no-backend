A language model only knows what was in its training data and what you put in the prompt. That leaves two gaps. The model does not know your private documents, and the model does not know new information from after training. When you ask about either one, the model might refuse, or worse, invent a confident answer. Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, closes those gaps by fetching relevant text at question time and adding that text to the prompt before the model answers.

The idea is simple: instead of hoping the answer is stored inside the model, you look up relevant information in a source you control and give that information to the model as context. The model's job shifts from remembering facts to reading the supplied text and writing a useful answer from it.

Why not fine-tune?

Fine-tuning teaches the model a new pattern by changing its internal parameters. That is useful for changing style or behavior, but it is a poor fit for knowledge that changes often. Fine-tuning is expensive to repeat, slow to update, and hard to audit. When the model gives an answer, you do not have an easy way to point at the exact source behind it.

RAG keeps knowledge outside the model, in a store you control and update. Adding a document becomes a write, not a training run. Removing or replacing stale information is also simpler. For knowledge that changes often, belongs to your organization, or needs citations, retrieval is usually a better fit than fine-tuning.

The pipeline

RAG has two phases. The first phase happens ahead of time, once per document. You split the source into chunks, turn each chunk into an embedding, and store those embeddings for search. The second phase happens at question time. You embed the question, retrieve the closest chunks, and pass those chunks to the model so it answers from the retrieved text.

Each stage has its own decisions and failure modes:

  • Chunking decides how to cut the source into pieces small enough to search, but large enough to make sense on their own.
  • Embeddings turn text into vectors, so text with similar meaning ends up close together. This makes search by meaning possible.
  • Retrieval takes the question, finds the most relevant chunks, and decides how many to keep.
  • Generation puts the retrieved text into a prompt and asks the model to answer from that text, ideally with citations.

Garbage in, garbage out

RAG does not make a model smarter. RAG changes what the model has in front of it. If retrieval returns the wrong chunks, the model answers from the wrong text and still sounds confident. That is why most of the work in a RAG system is retrieval quality, not the model itself.

Good answers depend on good inputs: clear source documents, sensible chunking, embeddings that capture meaning well, and retrieval that brings back the right context. When those parts work, even a smaller model gives grounded answers. When they fail, a stronger model still misleads.

A language model only knows what was in its training data and what you put in the prompt. That leaves two gaps. It does not know your private documents, and it does not know anything that happened after training. When you ask about either, the model either refuses or, worse, invents a confident answer. Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, closes both gaps by fetching relevant text at question time and putting it in the prompt before the model answers.

The idea is simple: instead of hoping the answer is baked into the model's weights, you look it up in a source you control and hand it to the model as context. The model's job shifts from remembering facts to reading the supplied text and writing a good answer from it.

Why not just fine-tune?

Fine-tuning bakes knowledge into the weights. It is expensive to repeat, slow to update, and hard to audit: you cannot easily point at where an answer came from. RAG keeps knowledge outside the model, in a store you can edit. Adding a document is a write, not a training run, and every answer can cite the exact text it used. For knowledge that changes often or needs attribution, retrieval usually beats fine-tuning.

The pipeline

RAG splits into two phases. The first happens ahead of time, once per document: split the source into chunks, turn each chunk into an embedding, and store the vectors. The second happens at question time: embed the question, retrieve the closest chunks, and pass them to the model to generate an answer.

Each stage has its own note, because each has its own decisions and failure modes:

  • Chunking decides how the source is cut into pieces small enough to retrieve but large enough to make sense on their own.
  • Embeddings turn text into vectors so that similar meaning lands in nearby points, which is what makes search by meaning possible.
  • Retrieval takes the question, finds the closest chunks, and decides how many and which ones to keep.
  • Generation assembles the retrieved text into a prompt and asks the model to answer from it, ideally with citations.

Garbage in, garbage out

RAG does not make a model smarter. It changes what the model has in front of it. If retrieval returns the wrong chunks, the model answers from the wrong text and sounds just as confident doing it. This is why most of the work in a RAG system is not the model at all. It is the retrieval quality: how the text is chunked, how well the embeddings capture meaning, and how the closest chunks are chosen. Get those right and a small model gives grounded answers. Get them wrong and the best model still misleads.

--- title: A Key That Never Expires Is a Risk That Never Ends heading: A Key That Never Expires Is a Risk That Never Ends listTitle: Prefer Credentials That Expire description: Why a long-lived stored credential is a permanent liability, why one that is issued on demand and expires on its own is a far smaller prize for an attacker, and why self-expiring beats self-remembered rotation. lang: en category: Infrastructure as Code date: 2027-05-14 dateText: May 14 tags: infrastructure-as-code, credentials, secrets, rotation, security ---

When one system needs to prove its identity to another, it uses a credential. There are two broad kinds, and which you choose has a large effect on your risk. One kind is long-lived: a key or password you create once, store somewhere, and keep using until you manually replace it. The other is short-lived: issued fresh when needed and expiring on its own soon after. Prefer the short-lived kind whenever you can.

The permanent liability

A stored, long-lived credential is a liability that never goes away on its own. It has to live somewhere to be reused, and anywhere it lives is somewhere it can leak from: a config file, a laptop, a backup, a log. And if it does leak, it stays useful to whoever found it until a human notices and revokes it, which might be much later, or never. Its power outlives your attention.

That is the core problem: a credential that never expires turns a single moment of exposure into an open-ended risk. The clock is never ticking against the attacker.

The smaller prize

A short-lived credential flips this. It is issued only when needed and expires by itself shortly after, so at any moment there is little to steal, and what there is becomes useless almost immediately. Even if one leaks, the window in which it works is tiny. An attacker who grabs it is holding something that is about to turn to dust.

There is a second, quieter benefit: nobody has to remember to replace it. Long-lived credentials rely on someone rotating them on schedule, which is exactly the kind of chore that slips. Short-lived ones rotate themselves by expiring, so the safe behavior happens automatically instead of depending on human diligence.

The general principle

The idea reaches beyond any particular tool. A secret that lasts forever is a permanent risk; a secret that expires is a temporary one. Wherever you can arrange for credentials to be handed out fresh and expire on their own, rather than stored and reused indefinitely, you shrink both the chance of a leak mattering and the damage when one happens. Storing a permanent key should feel like a last resort, not a default.

Worth remembering

Long-lived stored credentials are permanent liabilities: they sit somewhere they can leak, and stay dangerous until someone revokes them. Credentials that are issued on demand and expire on their own are a far smaller prize, useless almost as soon as they are taken, and they rotate themselves instead of relying on someone to remember. Prefer credentials that come and go over keys you keep forever.

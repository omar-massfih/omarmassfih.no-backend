When one service calls another over the network, that call can hang. The other side might be slow, overloaded, or stuck in a way that never sends back an answer or an error. How the caller handles that moment decides whether one slow dependency stays a small problem or drags the whole caller down with it.

Always set a deadline

A call with no time limit is a trap. If it never returns, it holds onto a thread, a connection, and memory the whole time. Enough stuck calls and the caller runs out of those resources and fails too, even though the caller itself is fine. So every network call needs a timeout: a deadline after which the caller gives up and moves on.

Be suspicious of any library that has no timeout, or defaults to waiting forever. It quietly turns a slow dependency into an unbounded wait. Picking the deadline is a trade-off, not a fixed number. Too short, and you give up on healthy-but-slow calls and cry wolf. Too long, and real failures tie up resources for ages. A reasonable method is to look at how long calls normally take and set the deadline a bit beyond that, so only the genuinely stuck ones get cut off.

Retry, but gently

Many failures are brief. A quick hiccup clears up on its own, so trying again after a short wait often just works, with no human involved. That is the case for retries.

But retrying carelessly can turn a small problem into a large one. If a dependency is failing because it is overloaded, retrying right away piles on more load exactly when it is already struggling, and pushes it further down. Two habits fix this. First, wait a little longer before each attempt instead of hammering at a fixed pace, so a struggling dependency gets room to recover. Second, add a bit of randomness to those waits. Otherwise every client that failed together retries together, in one synchronized wave that keeps knocking the dependency over just as it tries to get up.

Some failures should not be retried at all. If the error is permanent, like a rejected password, trying again only wastes effort. And retrying an action that is unsafe to repeat can cause real damage, such as charging a card twice.

Do not stack retries

Retries are dangerous when they pile up across layers. If A calls B calls C, and each layer retries, the attempts multiply: a few retries at the top can become a flood by the time they reach the bottom. The deepest, most stressed service gets the worst of it. The cleaner rule is to let one layer own the retrying and have the others fail fast, so the recovery effort does not become its own overload.

When to stop trying: the circuit breaker

Retries assume the next attempt might succeed soon. Sometimes that stops being true, and every call is just going to fail. Waiting through timeouts and retries for calls you know will fail only spreads the slowness back into the caller.

A circuit breaker handles this. Like the one in a house, it cuts the flow when things go wrong. While the dependency looks healthy, calls pass through. Once failures pile up, it flips open and calls fail instantly instead of hanging. After a while it lets a trickle through to test whether the dependency has recovered, and only fully reopens if those probes succeed. Retries and circuit breakers are partners: one keeps trying when success is near, the other stops trying when it is not.

One more thing to plan for. When the breaker opens on a feature that is not essential, the caller should carry on with a reduced experience rather than fail completely. Losing one optional feature is far better than taking down the whole page because of it.

Putting it together

Give every call a deadline. Retry only when another try might genuinely help, and space the tries out with a little randomness so recovery does not become a stampede. And know when to stop: sometimes failing fast, and gracefully dropping a non-essential feature, protects users better than trying one more time.

A service has a limit. Past some rate of requests it runs out of memory, threads, or connections, and its responses slow to a crawl. The dangerous part is what happens next: if it keeps accepting everything, it does not just get slow, it gets slow for everyone at once, and the whole thing becomes unusable. Handling that moment well is the difference between a busy service and a fallen one.

Say no quickly

The instinct under load is to try to serve every request. That is the wrong instinct. Once a service is at capacity, the kindest thing it can do is reject extra work fast, so the requests already in progress get the resources they need to finish. This is called load shedding: dropping some requests on purpose to keep the rest healthy.

Shedding can be smart about what it drops. If some requests matter more, or if a request has been waiting so long the user has probably given up, those are the ones to reject first, protecting the work most worth finishing. But shedding has a limit too: even rejecting a request costs a little effort, so if the flood keeps rising, eventually the cost of saying no can itself overwhelm the service.

Let a queue absorb the spikes

When callers do not need an instant answer, a queue helps. Instead of hitting the service directly, requests go into a channel, and the service pulls from it at its own comfortable pace. A short burst piles up briefly and then drains, so the spike never reaches the service at full force.

A queue smooths bumps, but it is not a cure for too much traffic. If requests keep arriving faster than they can be handled, the backlog just grows and grows until it becomes its own kind of failure. A queue buys time; only more capacity, added by hand or automatically, actually keeps up with a lasting rise in load.

Set limits per user

Load shedding reacts to one machine's stress in the moment. Rate limiting is different: it caps how much any single user or client may use over time, no matter how the machines are doing. It protects a service from one client, often a buggy or overeager one, quietly hogging everything by accident.

When you do turn a client away for going over its limit, tell it clearly that it hit a quota rather than sending a vague error. A well-behaved client that knows it was throttled will back off and try later, instead of treating the rejection as a random failure and retrying harder. Rate limits also double as a fairness and pricing tool: heavier users can be given, or charged for, larger quotas that match the capacity they consume.

Do a steady amount of work

A subtler idea helps a service stay calm under stress: try to do roughly the same amount of work no matter what is happening. A system whose effort barely changes with load has few surprise slow paths to trip over when things get busy. Doing a steady, slightly wasteful amount of work is often more reliable than doing the clever minimum, because steady behavior is easier to test and predict. Reserving a fixed amount of capacity up front fits the same idea: you can test the service at its true worst case, because its worst case is known.

The point

Under overload, protect the work you can finish instead of trying to accept everything. Shed excess quickly, let queues flatten short spikes, cap what any one client can take, and lean toward steady, predictable work. A service that knows how to say no gracefully stays useful for the people it can still serve.

Coordinating a group of machines is expensive when every machine has to talk to every other one to agree on anything. A common shortcut is to put a single machine in charge. It makes the decisions and sets the order; everyone else follows. This is a leader, and picking one is a standard move. But it comes with a specific, dangerous failure worth understanding.

Why a leader helps

With a leader, coordination gets simple. Instead of a noisy negotiation among everyone, there is one clear voice: the leader decides, the others accept. That cuts the amount of back-and-forth dramatically, and it gives you a single place that holds the authoritative order of things. For a lot of systems, that simplicity is the whole reason to elect a leader.

The danger: two leaders at once

Now suppose the network splits, so the group is cut into two pieces that cannot talk to each other. Each piece looks around, sees that the leader is unreachable, and concludes it needs to elect a new one. If both pieces go ahead, you now have two leaders, each convinced it is the one in charge.

This is called split brain, and it is genuinely destructive. Both leaders start accepting work and making decisions, unaware of each other. They hand out conflicting answers, accept clashing writes, and when the network heals you are left with two versions of reality that cannot be reconciled. One leader is helpful; two is worse than none.

The fix: only a majority may crown a leader

The cure is the same majority rule that underlies agreement in general: a machine may only become leader if more than half the group backs it. Because a split cannot produce two pieces that each hold more than half, at most one side can gather the votes to elect a leader. The other side, holding a minority, is unable to crown anyone and goes quiet until it can rejoin the majority.

So the majority requirement is not bureaucracy; it is exactly what stops the two halves of a partition from both electing a boss. It converts "each side does its own thing" into "at most one side may act."

Leaders are not free

Even done right, a leader has downsides to plan for. It is a bottleneck, since work funnels through it, and a single point of failure, so you need a way to notice when it dies and elect a replacement. When one leader cannot keep up, a common answer is to split the data into independent groups, each with its own leader, so the coordinating load is shared rather than piled on one machine.

The takeaway

Electing a leader makes coordination cheap by giving the group one voice. The hazard is a network split producing two leaders who corrupt each other's work, a split brain. Requiring a majority to elect a leader prevents it, because two halves cannot both be a majority. And remember a leader is a bottleneck and a weak point, so detect its failure and, when needed, divide the work across several leaders.

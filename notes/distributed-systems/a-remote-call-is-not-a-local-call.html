It is tempting to make calling another service look exactly like calling an ordinary function in your own program: same familiar syntax, the network tucked out of sight. It feels clean. But that tidiness hides differences that matter, and they tend to reappear as production surprises.

A local call always comes back

When you call a function inside your own program, it runs and returns. Barring a crash that takes the whole program down with it, there is no world where you call it and simply never hear back. You do not worry about the call getting lost on the way there, or its answer getting lost on the way back.

A call across the network has all of those failure modes and more. The request can be lost before it arrives. The reply can be lost after the work is done. The other machine can be slow, so the answer takes seconds instead of an instant. Or it can be down entirely. None of this can happen to a local call, and all of it is routine over a network.

The worst part: not knowing

The hardest failure is not that something went wrong; it is that you often cannot tell what went wrong. When you send a request and hear nothing, you cannot distinguish between two very different situations: the other side never received the request, or it received it, did the work, and its reply got lost on the way back. From where you stand, both look identical: silence.

That uncertainty is exactly why simply retrying is dangerous. If the work already happened and only the reply was lost, retrying does the work a second time. For something like a payment, that is real harm. Retrying is only safe when the action can be repeated without extra effect, so this is a place where designing actions to be safely repeatable pays off directly.

Why the disguise backfires

When a remote call is dressed up to look local, it is easy to forget all of this. You write code as if the call always returns promptly and exactly once, because that is what local calls do. Then, in production, the lost messages, timeouts, and duplicate effects arrive as baffling bugs, precisely because the code was written as though they could not happen.

Putting it together

A call over the network is not a normal function call, however much it is made to look like one. It can be lost, delayed, or left unanswered, and often you cannot tell whether it succeeded. Treat it as a message that might not arrive, to a partner that might not answer, whose outcome you might not learn, and design for retries by making the work safe to repeat. Respect the network, and it surprises you far less.

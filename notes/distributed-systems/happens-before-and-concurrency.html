When two people change the same thing in a system at once, the system has to figure out what to do. To answer that, it first needs a clear idea of what "at once" even means. In everyday life we would check the clock. In a distributed system, the clock turns out to be the wrong tool.

Clocks disagree, so do not trust them

Different machines have slightly different clocks, and even tiny differences make timestamps unreliable for deciding order. A write stamped one millisecond earlier might have actually happened after another, or the two machines might simply disagree about the time. Ordering events by their timestamps quietly builds on a shaky foundation.

So distributed systems use a different, sturdier idea. Instead of asking when two actions happened, they ask whether one action could have known about the other.

The "knew about it" test

One action happens before another if the second one could have been aware of the first: it read its result, built on it, or reacted to it. If you reply to a message, your reply happens after the message, because your reply depended on having seen it. There is a clear chain of cause and effect.

Two actions are concurrent when neither one knew about the other. This has nothing to do with the clock. Two writes made an hour apart are still concurrent if the later writer had no way of knowing about the earlier one. And two writes in the very same second are not concurrent if one clearly built on the other. Concurrency is about awareness, not timing.

Why this distinction matters

The distinction decides how the system should treat a pair of writes. If one write happened before the other, the later one is simply the newer version, and it can safely replace the older one. There is no conflict, just an update.

But if two writes are concurrent, neither is "newer" in any meaningful sense. They are two independent opinions about the same data. Silently keeping only one of them, for example by picking whichever has the later timestamp, quietly throws away real work someone did. Both edits were valid; one just vanishes.

Keeping both, then merging

A safer approach keeps both concurrent versions instead of discarding one, and hands them to the application to combine sensibly. Think of two people adding items to a shared shopping cart at the same time: the right answer is usually a cart with both items, not one person's cart winning and the other's disappearing. The system cannot know the right merge on its own, so it preserves both and lets the application decide.

To do this it needs to track which writes knew about which, so it can tell genuine updates from true conflicts. That bookkeeping is what lets it say, with confidence, "these two really were concurrent," rather than guessing from clocks.

The upshot

In a distributed system, "at the same time" means "neither knew about the other," not "close together on the clock." Get that right and the system can tell a plain update from a real conflict, replace the old safely, and preserve both when two changes truly collided, instead of losing someone's work to a timestamp.

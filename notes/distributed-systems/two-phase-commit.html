Inside one database, a transaction can make several changes all-or-nothing. But what if a single action has to change data in several separate databases at once, and you need them all to commit together or all to abort together, never some of each? No single database can enforce that, because each only controls itself. The usual answer is a two-step handshake.

The handshake

One machine acts as coordinator and runs the change in two phases.

First, it asks each database the same question: "are you able to commit your part?" Each one checks, and if it can, it makes a durable promise: yes, I can commit, and I will not back out on my own. If any database says no, the whole thing will be called off.

Second, once the coordinator has heard back from everyone, it announces the verdict. If all said yes, it tells them all to commit. If any said no, it tells them all to abort. Because everyone already promised in the first phase, they can all carry out the verdict together. When it works, it delivers exactly the all-or-nothing outcome you wanted.

The crack: a crash at the wrong moment

Now the flaw. Picture the coordinator collecting "yes" from every database, and then crashing before it announces the verdict. Each database is now in an agonizing spot. It promised it could commit and swore not to abort on its own, so it cannot just give up. But it also never heard whether the final decision was commit or abort. It is stuck holding its promise, unable to move either way.

All it can do is wait for the coordinator to come back and tell it the outcome. While it waits, it is holding resources and cannot finish, effectively frozen. This is why the handshake is called a blocking approach: a crash at that one precise moment can leave the participants waiting indefinitely.

Why one straggler freezes everyone

The same fragility shows up if any single database becomes unreachable during the handshake. Since the outcome has to be unanimous, the coordinator cannot safely declare commit while one participant is silent, so the whole transaction stalls on the slowest or sickest member. One unavailable part is enough to hold up all the others.

Why it is still used

Given that flaw, why is this handshake everywhere? Because when things are healthy it is simple, cheap, and easy to reason about. It takes few messages, it is straightforward to implement, and the blocking case, a crash at the exact wrong instant, is rare. For many systems, that simplicity is worth the risk of an occasional stall, especially with a coordinator that recovers quickly.

The bottom line

To make several databases commit as one, ask them all "can you?", collect their promises, then tell them all the verdict. It gives clean all-or-nothing results when everyone is up. Its weakness is blocking: if the coordinator dies after the promises but before the verdict, participants are stuck waiting, and one unreachable member can freeze the rest. Simple and cheap in the good case, stuck in the bad one, which is the trade it makes.

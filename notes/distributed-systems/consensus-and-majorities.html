Sometimes a group of machines has to agree on a single answer: who the leader is, what order events happened in, whether a value is final. This is called consensus, and it sounds like it should be easy. It is not, and understanding why reveals one of the most useful ideas in distributed systems.

The core difficulty

The trouble is that you can never be sure who is alive. When you send a message to another machine and hear nothing back, you cannot tell whether it has crashed or is just slow, or whether the network is delaying the reply. A dead machine and a very slow one look identical from the outside.

This is a real bind. You must not wait forever for a machine that may be dead, but you also must not wrongly declare a slow machine dead and act without it. If agreement required hearing from everyone, a single silent machine could freeze the whole group indefinitely.

The trick: don't require everyone

The way out is to stop requiring everyone and require only a majority: more than half the group. If more than half agree on something, that decision stands, and the group can move on without waiting for the silent ones.

Why is that safe? Because any two majorities of the same group must overlap. Take more than half the members twice, in any two ways, and at least one member is in both. That shared member is the guard: it will not go along with two conflicting decisions. So it is impossible for the group to agree to two different answers, because there is no way to form two majorities that do not share someone who would object.

What this buys, and what it costs

This overlap is quietly powerful. It means the group can keep making decisions as long as more than half its members are reachable, tolerating the rest being crashed or cut off. You trade "wait for everyone" for "wait for most," and gain the ability to make progress despite failures.

The cost is that you must actually be able to reach a majority. If too many machines are down or partitioned away, no majority can form, and the group correctly refuses to decide rather than risk a split answer. It also means that to survive a certain number of failures, you need enough machines that a majority still exists after those failures happen. Surviving more failures means running more machines.

Worth remembering

Agreement is hard because you can never be certain who is alive; silence could mean death or just delay. The escape is to decide by majority instead of unanimity. Because any two majorities share a member, the group can never commit to two conflicting answers, and it can keep going as long as most members are reachable. "More than half" turns an impossible wait into a safe, workable rule.

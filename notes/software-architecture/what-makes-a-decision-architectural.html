People use "architecture" and "design" loosely, but there is a useful line between them, and knowing where a decision falls tells you how much care it deserves. Not every choice is a big one. The trick is telling which are.

Three questions

Three questions help place a decision on the scale from ordinary design to genuine architecture.

  • Is it strategic or tactical? A tactical decision is local and short-term: how to write this one function. A strategic one is long-lived and broad, shaping how much of the system fits together. The more strategic, the more architectural.
  • How hard is it to change later? Something you can adjust in an afternoon is design. Something that would take months to unwind, because everything else came to depend on it, is architecture. Difficulty of change is one of the clearest signals.
  • How big are the trade-offs? If the choice barely affects anything beyond itself, it is design. If it swings important qualities across the whole system, speed against flexibility, simplicity against scale, it is architecture.

The more a decision is long-lasting, expensive to reverse, and heavy with trade-offs, the more it belongs on the architecture end.

Why the sorting matters

This is not labeling for its own sake. It tells you where to spend your attention. The architectural decisions, the strategic, hard-to-undo, trade-off-heavy ones, deserve real deliberation, because getting them wrong is expensive and lasting. The design decisions can be made quickly and revised freely, because they are cheap to change.

Spending days agonizing over an easily reversible detail, while rushing a choice that will shape the system for years, gets this exactly backwards. Sort first, then spend your care where the stakes actually are.

Why breadth beats depth here

This also explains something about the skill involved. Making good architectural choices is mostly about comparing options and picking well, not about being the deepest expert in any one tool. Knowing a little about many approaches lets you weigh alternatives you would not otherwise consider. Deep mastery of a single technology is valuable, but it tempts you to force that one tool onto every problem. The person choosing between paths benefits more from knowing the map than from knowing one road perfectly.

Putting it together

Tell architecture from design by asking whether a decision is strategic, hard to change, and heavy with trade-offs. The more it is all three, the more careful thought it earns; the less it is, the faster you should move. Sorting decisions this way puts your deliberation where being wrong is costly, and lets you be quick everywhere else.

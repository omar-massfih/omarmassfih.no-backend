"Coupling" is a vague word. Two pieces of code are coupled when they depend on each other, but that covers everything from a shared name to a fragile dependence on exact timing. Connascence is a sharper word for the same idea. Two pieces of code are connascent when changing one forces you to change the other to keep the program correct. It gives us language to say not just that things are coupled, but how badly.

Some couplings are easier to change than others

Not all dependencies are equally painful. The simplest is when two places just have to agree on a name: rename a function, and every caller must use the new name. That is annoying but easy, because a tool can find and fix every use.

Harder ones hide. When two places must agree on the meaning of a value, like both sides "knowing" that a status of 2 means cancelled, nothing points from one to the other. When correctness depends on the order of arguments, or on two things happening in the right sequence at the right time, the dependency is invisible until it breaks. These looser, runtime dependencies are the ones that bite, because you cannot see them by reading a single spot.

Three things that decide how much a coupling hurts

Whether a given coupling is a problem depends on three things. How hard it is to change: agreeing on a name is easy, agreeing on hidden timing is hard. How far apart the coupled pieces sit: two lines in the same small function can depend on each other all they like, but two services across the system agreeing on a secret convention is dangerous. And how many pieces are involved: a convention shared by three places is easier to fix than one shared by three hundred.

These combine in a natural way. A strong, hard-to-change coupling is fine if it is tucked inside one small, close unit. The same coupling stretched across distant parts of a system, touching many of them, is where real fragility lives.

Three rules that follow

From this you get three simple rules of thumb:

  • Keep tight coupling close. Inside a single module, where the pieces live together, stronger dependencies are acceptable and often unavoidable.
  • Keep coupling across boundaries loose. Where separate modules or services meet, prefer the easy-to-change kinds of dependency, like a plain agreed name, over hidden conventions or timing.
  • Turn strong couplings into weaker ones where you can. If two distant parts secretly agree that "2 means cancelled," give it a real name they both refer to, so the dependency becomes visible and easy to follow.

The takeaway

Connascence turns a fuzzy worry about "too much coupling" into questions you can actually answer: how hard is this to change, how far apart are the pieces, and how many are there? Aim to keep the tightest dependencies small and local, and to keep whatever crosses a boundary as easy to change as possible. That is most of what good modularity comes down to.

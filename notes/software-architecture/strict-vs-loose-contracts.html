When two systems exchange data, there is an agreement between them about what that data looks like: which fields exist, what types they are, which are required. That agreement is a contract, and how tightly you specify it is a real dial with real consequences. You can make it strict or loose, and neither is simply better.

A strict contract

A strict contract pins everything down: exact field names, exact types, which fields must be present, even the order. Both sides commit to the precise shape.

The upside is confidence. Because everything is specified, tools can check that both sides match before anything even runs, and a mismatch is caught early rather than blowing up in production. You know exactly what you are getting. The downside is brittleness: since every detail is fixed, any change to the shape forces every system that depends on it to change in lockstep. Add a field, and even the consumers who do not care about it may have to update just to stay compatible.

A loose contract

A loose contract specifies very little, often just named values, and lets each side take what it needs. There is no rigid schema policing the exchange.

The upside is independence. A system can change its internals, or add unrelated data, without breaking the systems that consume it, because each consumer reads only the specific fields it uses and ignores everything else. Things can evolve on their own schedules. The downside is that mistakes are no longer caught for you: a misspelled name, a missing value, an unexpected type can slip through silently, because nothing is automatically checking that both sides still line up.

The dial

So the choice is a genuine trade-off. Strict buys you early, automatic error-catching at the price of everyone having to move together. Loose buys you free, independent evolution at the price of policing mismatches yourself, usually with your own tests. One end optimizes for safety and verification; the other for decoupling and change.

Where to set the dial depends on your situation. If the two sides change rarely and you value ironclad verification, lean strict. If they change often, are maintained by different teams, or must evolve without constant coordination, lean loose, and invest in tests that confirm each consumer still gets what it needs.

The bottom line

Contract strictness is a dial between "catch every mismatch, but couple everyone together" and "let everyone evolve freely, but catch mismatches yourself." Strict contracts give confidence and early checking at the cost of lockstep change; loose ones give independent evolution at the cost of your own vigilance. Set the dial by how often things change and how much coordination you can afford.

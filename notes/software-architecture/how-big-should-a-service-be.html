When you break a system into services, you have to decide how big each one should be. Splitting into more, smaller pieces is not automatically better, and neither is keeping things large. The right size comes from weighing two opposing sets of forces, not from following a slogan.

Why "one responsibility" is not enough

A popular rule says each service should do one thing. It sounds clean, but it hides a problem: what counts as "one thing" is a matter of opinion. Draw the line narrowly and you get lots of tiny services; draw it broadly and you get a few large ones. The slogan does not tell you where to draw it, so on its own it tends to push people toward splitting more than they should.

Forces that pull a system apart

There are real reasons to split a piece off into its own service:

  • Different change rates. If one part changes constantly while the rest is stable, separating it means you can update and test the volatile part without touching everything else.
  • Different scaling needs. If one part gets hammered with traffic and another barely runs, splitting lets each scale on its own instead of scaling the quiet part just to keep up with the busy one.
  • Failure isolation. If one part crashing would otherwise take unrelated features down with it, separating it keeps its failures contained.
  • Security. Sensitive operations can be walled off behind their own service with stricter access.

Forces that pull it back together

Pulling in the other direction are equally real reasons to keep things together:

  • Shared all-or-nothing work. If two operations must succeed or fail as one unit, that is far simpler inside a single service, where one transaction can cover both.
  • Constant back-and-forth. If two split services would have to talk to each other on almost every request, the chatter can cost more than the split ever saved, in both speed and reliability.
  • Shared business logic. If services keep needing the same rules, a change to those rules forces you to update and retest all of them together anyway.
  • Tangled data. If one service constantly needs data another one owns, every access becomes a cross-service request, adding coordination you did not have before.

Weigh, do not chant

Sizing a service means putting these forces side by side for the case in front of you and deciding which matter most. A part that changes often but must share an all-or-nothing transaction with its neighbor might be better left where it is. And splitting is not the only tool: sometimes you can keep different-paced logic in one deployable service but tidy it into separate internal components, getting the organization without the cost of another service to run and coordinate.

The takeaway

Do not size services by a rule of thumb. Some forces pull the system apart: different change rates, different scaling, failure isolation, security. Others pull it together: shared transactions, chatty workflows, shared logic, tangled data. Good boundaries come from weighing those forces honestly, and remembering that not every separation needs to become a separate service.

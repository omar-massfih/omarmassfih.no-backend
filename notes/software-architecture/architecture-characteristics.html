Requirements tell you what a system must do: take orders, show a dashboard, send a receipt. But whether the system actually succeeds depends on a second set of things that the requirements often skip: how fast it responds, how much downtime is acceptable, how easily it can be changed, how well it copes as usage grows. These qualities are the architecture characteristics, and shaping the system around them is much of what architecture is.

The "how well," not the "what"

It helps to separate the two clearly. The "what" is the behavior: the features. The characteristics are the "how well": fast enough, available enough, secure enough, easy enough to change, able to scale. A system can do everything on the feature list and still fail because it falls over under load, or because every small change takes weeks. The characteristics decide whether the features survive contact with the real world.

The most important ones are often unwritten

Here is a trap. Many crucial characteristics are never written down, because they are simply assumed. Nobody adds "the site should still work when lots of people use it" to the requirements; they take it for granted. These unspoken expectations are easy to miss precisely because no document points at them, and missing one can sink a project as surely as missing a feature.

So part of the job is to go looking for the characteristics nobody stated but everybody expects, and make them explicit before they become nasty surprises.

You cannot have them all

The bigger lesson is that these qualities fight each other. Every one you decide to support adds cost and complexity, and pushing one up often drags another down. Making a system extremely secure can make it slower and harder to use. Making it handle enormous scale can make it more complex and harder to change. There is no design that maxes out everything at once.

This is why chasing every desirable quality is a mistake. You pick the few that matter most for this particular system and accept that others will be merely good enough. The honest goal is not a perfect architecture but the least bad balance of competing qualities for your situation.

Being wrong more cheaply

Since you are choosing among trade-offs with incomplete information, you will get some of it wrong. That is far less scary if the architecture is easy to change. The easier it is to adjust later, the less pressure there is to pick every characteristic perfectly on the first try. Flexibility turns a high-stakes guess into a decision you can revisit.

What to remember

A system succeeds not just on what it does but on how well it does it: its speed, availability, changeability, security, and ability to scale. Hunt down the important qualities nobody bothered to write, accept that you cannot maximize them all, and deliberately choose the few that matter most. Then keep the design changeable, so being partly wrong is cheap to fix.

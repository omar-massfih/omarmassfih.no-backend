Inside a single database, you can wrap several changes in one transaction so they all succeed or all fail together. It is a wonderful safety net. The moment a workflow spreads across several services, each with its own database, that net is gone. Each service can commit its own step, but nothing makes all the steps happen as one unit. So what do you do when step three fails after steps one and two already succeeded?

The problem in plain terms

Say placing an order reserves stock, charges a card, then books shipping. The first two succeed and commit. Then shipping fails. You cannot simply "roll back," because the stock reservation and the charge are already done, in separate systems, as far as each of them is concerned. The workflow is stuck halfway, and half-done is exactly the state the single transaction used to prevent. There are two honest ways out.

Option one: undo what was done

The first is to walk the finished steps backward and undo them: refund the charge, release the stock. Each undo is its own action that reverses an earlier one.

This is harder than it sounds. Because each step already committed, the undo is not a clean rollback but a new operation that tries to reverse the effect, and it can fail too. Worse, something may have already acted on the change before you undid it: a confirmation email went out, another process saw the reservation. You are not rewinding time, you are patching over things that really happened, and the patch itself is not guaranteed.

Option two: push forward and repair

The second way accepts that the workflow will be briefly inconsistent and focuses on getting it finished. Instead of undoing, the system keeps track of where the workflow got to and works to complete it: retry the shipping step, or flag it for someone to handle, until the whole thing reaches a proper finished state. The user can often be told "done" right away, while the remaining tightening-up happens in the background.

The thing that makes this work is knowing the workflow's state at all times. You must be able to say whether an order is complete, still in progress, failed, being retried, or waiting for a human. Without that, a half-finished workflow just gets lost. With it, "briefly inconsistent" becomes a state you manage on purpose rather than a bug.

The trade-off

Insisting on strict all-or-nothing across services is possible but expensive: it ties the services tightly together, makes everyone wait, and pushes the complexity of undoing partial work onto you. Accepting eventual consistency keeps the system responsive and loosely joined, but you take on the job of tracking workflow state and living with short windows where things do not yet line up. Neither is free; you are choosing which cost to pay.

In short

Splitting a workflow across services removes the single transaction that made "all or nothing" easy. You can undo committed steps, which is fiddly and can fail because the world already moved on, or you can push forward and repair, which stays responsive but demands that you always know the workflow's state. Distributed consistency is not something you turn on; it is something you design.

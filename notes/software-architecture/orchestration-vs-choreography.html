Once a task is spread across several services, something has to make them work together in the right order. Placing an order might mean checking stock, charging a card, and booking a shipment, each handled by a different service. There are two very different ways to coordinate that, and the choice shapes how the whole system feels.

The conductor

The first way puts one service in charge, like a conductor leading an orchestra. It calls the stock service, then the payment service, then the shipping service, watching each result and deciding what happens next. All the workflow logic lives in that one place.

The big advantage is clarity. If you want to know how the process works, you read the conductor. If something goes wrong, there is one place that knows the whole story and can decide how to recover. This central control is comfortable and easy to reason about.

The cost is that the conductor becomes a bottleneck and a weak point. Every workflow runs through it, so it has to keep up with all of them, and if it is down, nothing coordinates. The services also lean on it heavily, which ties them more tightly together.

The dance

The second way has no conductor at all. Each service simply reacts to things that happen and announces what it did, and the next service picks up from there, like dancers who know their steps and respond to each other without anyone calling the moves. The stock service announces "reserved," which prompts the payment service, whose "charged" announcement prompts shipping.

This is loose and scales well. No single service is in charge, so there is no central bottleneck, and each part can work at its own pace. The services depend on each other far less directly.

The cost is that the workflow is now scattered. No single place describes the whole process; it is an emergent result of everyone's reactions. That makes it harder to see what the overall flow is, and harder to follow when something goes wrong, because the story is spread across many services.

Which one a job needs

The choice is a straight trade-off between control and looseness. A complex workflow with lots of branches, error paths, and steps that must happen in a precise order benefits from a conductor, because you want one place holding all that logic. A simple, mostly straight-line flow that needs to handle huge volume benefits from the dance, because you want looseness and scale and there is not much complicated logic to centralize.

The bottom line

To coordinate work across services, you either appoint a conductor or let the services dance. The conductor gives you visibility and control at the price of a bottleneck and tighter coupling. The dance gives you looseness and scale at the price of a workflow you cannot see in one place. Match the choice to the workflow: the more complex the coordination, the more a conductor earns its keep.

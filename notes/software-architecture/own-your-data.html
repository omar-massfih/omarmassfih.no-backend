Suppose you have split a system into pieces and want them to stay genuinely independent. There is one rule that does most of the work: each piece owns its own data, and no other piece is allowed to reach directly into it. If one piece needs something from another, it asks through a defined interface. It does not open the other's database and read its tables.

Why direct access ruins independence

The moment one piece reads another's tables directly, it becomes tied to the exact shape of that data: the table names, the columns, the types. That may seem harmless, it is just reading, but it quietly welds the two together.

Now the owner cannot reorganize its own storage without breaking the reader. Rename a column, restructure a table, and something you did not even know was looking suddenly fails. This is precisely the tangle that made a shared database painful in the first place, and reaching into someone else's tables recreates it. You will have done the work of splitting and kept the coupling anyway.

Ownership buys freedom to change

Owning your data means you are free to change how you store it, as long as the interface you expose to others stays the same. You can rename columns, split or merge tables, even switch to an entirely different kind of database underneath, and nobody outside notices, because nobody outside was ever looking at your storage.

This is why the interface you expose should deliberately not mirror your tables. Its job is to hide them. It presents a stable, sensible view of what you provide, while your actual storage is free to shift around behind it. The interface is a promise about what others can rely on; everything behind it is yours to change.

The front door, not the back window

The mental picture is simple. Your data lives inside your house. Others are welcome to what you offer, but they come through the front door you built and controlled, an interface you designed, not by climbing through a back window straight into your rooms. The front door lets you rearrange the furniture whenever you like; the back window ties your hands.

What to remember

Independence between the pieces of a system comes from each piece owning its data and hiding it behind an interface it controls. Let another piece read your tables directly and you are coupled to your own schema forever, unable to change it without breaking them. Expose a stable interface instead, keep your storage private behind it, and you stay free to change how you store things without anyone else having to care.

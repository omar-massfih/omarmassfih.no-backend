When starting a new system, there is a strong pull toward immediately splitting it into many small, independently deployed pieces, because that is what big, admired systems look like. It is usually the wrong first move, and understanding why saves a lot of pain.

The monolith's real virtue is simplicity

A monolith, a single deployable unit that holds everything, is simpler to build, understand, test, and deploy, and it is cheaper. For a new or small system, that simplicity is exactly the asset you want. You can move fast and change your mind cheaply while you are still discovering what the system even is. There is one thing to run, one thing to reason about.

Splitting early buys what you don't need yet

Breaking the system into many pieces up front buys you operational abilities, scaling each piece on its own, deploying them separately, isolating failures between them, at a steep price in complexity. Early on, you usually do not need those abilities, so you are paying the cost without collecting the benefit.

And there is a deeper trap. Splitting forces you to decide the boundaries between the pieces before you understand the domain, and early guesses about boundaries are usually wrong. A wrong boundary inside a monolith is a quick refactor. A wrong boundary between separately deployed pieces is painful to move, because now the mistake is spread across deployment, data, and coordination. You are locking in guesses at the worst possible time to be guessing.

The weaknesses arrive later

None of this means the monolith is flawless. Its real weaknesses are genuine: you scale and deploy it as one whole rather than in parts, and a bad failure in one corner can take the entire thing down. But notice that these problems show up later, once the system is large, heavily loaded, and worked on by many people. They are the problems of success, and you can address them when they actually start to hurt.

Keep the seams clean

The healthy path is to start as a monolith but keep clean boundaries inside it, so the pieces are separable in principle. Then, when the pain of scaling, deploying, or coordinating genuinely justifies it, you split, and by that point you actually understand where the seams should go. The split is earned by real need and informed by real experience, instead of guessed at the start.

Worth remembering

Do not buy the complexity of many independently deployed pieces before you need it. Start with a monolith for its simplicity and low cost, keep tidy internal boundaries so a future split is possible, and split only when the system is big enough that the monolith's real weaknesses start to bite. By then you will know where to cut, which is exactly what you could not know at the beginning.

Changing data that lives on disk is riskier than it looks. A single logical change, moving money, updating a record, often takes several smaller steps to fully land in durable storage. If the power dies partway through, you are left with something half-applied: not the old state, not the new state, but a broken in-between that is worse than either.

The core trick

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple. Before you touch the real data, first write down what you are about to do. You append a short record of the intended change to a log: a running, append-only list on disk. Only once that record is safely stored do you go and make the actual change to the data.

Appending to the end of a log is quick and hard to interrupt, so this "note your intent first" step is cheap. And it changes everything about what a crash means.

Why it makes crashes survivable

Suppose the machine dies mid-change. On restart, you read back the log and use it to put things right. For changes whose intent was fully recorded and meant to stick, you re-apply them to make sure they actually took hold. For changes that were only partly done, you undo them, rolling back to a clean state. The log is the authority the system trusts to decide what really happened.

This is also what "your change is committed" really means. It does not mean the data has been fully written everywhere. It means your intent is safely in the log, from which the system can always finish the job, even after a crash. Commit is a promise backed by the log, not by the data being everywhere yet.

The same idea shows up everywhere

Once you see the pattern, you notice it all over. Record what you are going to do before you do it, so an interruption can be recovered from the record. Filesystems use it to survive power loss. Accounting has done it for centuries: the ledger of intended entries comes before, and outlives, any particular tally. Anytime an operation could be interrupted midway, a durable note of intent is cheap insurance.

The bottom line

A change that takes several steps can be left broken if a crash lands in the middle. Writing down what you intend to do, in a durable log, before you do it, means a crash is no longer a disaster: you replay the log to finish what was meant to stick and undo what was not. Record intent first, act second, and recovery becomes possible instead of hopeless.

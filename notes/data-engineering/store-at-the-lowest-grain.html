When you decide how to store data, you decide how detailed each record is. You could store one record per individual sale, or one record per day holding just that day's total. This level of detail has a name: the grain. Choosing it is one of the most consequential and easily underestimated decisions in working with data.

Detail only flows one way

Here is the asymmetry that makes this matter. If you keep every individual sale, you can always add them up into a daily total, a weekly total, a total per product, whatever you need. Fine detail rolls up into any summary you like.

But it does not work in reverse. If you only stored the daily total, you can never recover the individual sales that made it up. The detail is gone. You cannot ask "what time of day do people buy?" or "which products sold together?" because those answers lived in the detail you threw away. Summaries flow out of detail, but detail never flows back out of a summary.

Why the temptation is real

Storing summaries is tempting because it is smaller, cheaper, and faster to query. If today you only ever look at daily totals, keeping every sale feels wasteful. The catch is that you are deciding, permanently, that you will never want to ask a more detailed question. And the questions people want to ask almost always grow over time.

This is why the safe default is to store data at the finest grain you reasonably can. You are not just saving today's numbers; you are keeping future questions answerable. The extra space is the price of not painting yourself into a corner.

Summaries still have a place

None of this means you must query raw detail every time. You can, and often should, build summary tables on top for speed, so common questions run fast without crunching everything from scratch. The key is that those summaries are built from detail you still keep, not a replacement for it. Keep the detail underneath, and add fast summaries above it.

In short

The detail you store is a one-way door: you can always summarize it later, but you can never rebuild it once it is gone. Store data at the finest grain you can afford, and build summaries on top for convenience. It costs more space today, and it keeps tomorrow's questions from becoming unanswerable, which is almost always the better trade.

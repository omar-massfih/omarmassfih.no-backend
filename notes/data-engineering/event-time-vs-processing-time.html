When you process a stream of events, sales, clicks, sensor readings, there are two different times attached to each one, and mixing them up quietly ruins your numbers. Keeping them straight is one of the most practical ideas in working with streams.

Two clocks

The first time is when the event actually happened: the moment of the sale, the click, the reading. Call it event time; it is usually stamped on the event when it is created. The second is when your system got around to handling it. Call it processing time.

In a perfect world these would be nearly the same. In the real world they drift apart. Events get delayed on the network, wait in a queue, or get reprocessed after a restart. An event that happened at noon might not reach your processor until 12:05, or until tomorrow if something was backed up.

Why the wrong clock lies

Suppose you want to count sales per hour. If you count by processing time, by when you handled each event, you get a distorted picture whenever the two clocks diverge. If your processor was down for an hour and then caught up all at once, all those delayed events get handled together and show up as a giant spike, even though the real sales were spread out evenly. The outage itself looks like an hour with no sales at all. Both the spike and the dip are inventions of your processing schedule, not things that happened in the world.

So for any question about when things really occurred, you must use event time. Group events by when they happened, not by when you happened to process them, and the picture reflects reality regardless of delays and restarts.

The catch: you never know if everyone has arrived

Using event time raises a hard question. To count "sales between noon and one," you need all the events from that hour. But an event from that hour might still be stuck somewhere and arrive late, after you thought the hour was done. These late arrivals are stragglers, and you can never be fully certain none are still coming.

That forces a choice. You can decide that once an hour's window is closed, you ignore anything that shows up late, accepting a slightly incomplete count. Or you can keep the window open, or revise the answer, emitting a correction when late events arrive. Neither is free: one trades completeness for a firm answer, the other trades a firm answer for completeness.

The point

Every event carries two times: when it happened and when you processed it. Analyzing by processing time invents spikes and dips that reflect your schedule, not reality, so use event time for anything about when things occurred. Just accept that you can never be sure all of a past window's events have arrived, and decide in advance whether you will ignore latecomers or revise your answer for them.

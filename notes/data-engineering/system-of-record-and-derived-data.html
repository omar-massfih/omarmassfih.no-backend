Real systems keep the same information in several places: the main database, a cache, a search index, a recommendation list, a summary table for reports. It is easy to treat these as equals. They are not, and drawing a clear line between them prevents a whole class of painful bugs.

Source of truth versus copies

Pick one place as the source of truth: where a new fact is first written and officially recorded. Everything else is derived data, computed from that source. A search index is derived from the records it indexes. A cache is derived from whatever it caches. A daily summary is derived from the raw events.

The useful thing about this split is that it tells you what is precious and what is not. The source is irreplaceable; lose it and the facts are gone. Everything derived is, by definition, rebuildable: if your search index is corrupted, you can throw it away and regenerate it from the source. Derived data is redundant on purpose, kept around for speed and convenience, but never the thing you truly cannot lose.

The dual-write trap

Here is where teams get hurt. Suppose that when something changes, you write it directly into two systems yourself, say the database and the search index, one after the other. It seems reasonable, and it quietly breaks.

If the process crashes between the two writes, one system has the change and the other does not, and they stay that way. If two updates to the same thing race, they might land in the two systems in different orders, so each ends up believing something different. Now your two systems permanently disagree, and nothing tells you which one is right. This is the dual-write problem, and it does not fix itself.

Derive instead of dual-writing

The way out follows straight from the source-of-truth idea. Do not write the fact into two places by hand. Write it once, to the source, and let the other systems follow the source's stream of changes to update themselves. Because they all derive from one place, in one order, they cannot drift into permanent disagreement. If one falls behind or breaks, it catches up from the source rather than holding its own conflicting version.

This also makes recovery calm. A derived system that gets corrupted or needs a new shape is just rebuilt from the source's history. The source holds the truth; the copies are always reconstructable views of it.

Putting it together

Name one source of truth and treat everything else as derived: redundant, disposable, rebuildable. That tells you what to protect and what you can always regenerate. And resist writing the same fact into two systems directly, because a crash or a race leaves them disagreeing forever. Write once to the source, let the rest follow, and the pieces stay in step.

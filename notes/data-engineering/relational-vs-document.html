When you store data, you choose a shape for it. Two shapes dominate, and they take opposite approaches. One breaks data into linked tables; the other keeps related data bundled together. Neither is better in general, but one usually fits a given problem better, and the way your data connects tells you which.

Tables that reference each other

The first approach splits data into separate tables and links them by reference. A fact lives in exactly one place, and everything that needs it points to it by an identifier rather than repeating it.

The big win is no duplication. If a country's name or a customer's address lives in one row, and everything else refers to it, then changing it means changing one place. Using a stable identifier to point at it helps here too: the identifier never changes even if the human-readable text does, so references never go stale. Storing each fact once, and pointing at it, keeps the data from quietly contradicting itself.

Documents that stand alone

The second approach keeps related data together in one self-contained record: a customer with their addresses and orders all nested inside, like a little tree. To read the whole thing, you fetch one record and it is all there, no stitching together from many tables.

This shines when your data really is shaped like a tree, one thing owning many smaller things, and when you usually want the whole thing at once. It is all in one place, so reading it is one simple fetch. The costs are the mirror of the other approach: you load the entire record even when you only need a corner of it, and any fact shared across many records gets copied into all of them, so updating that fact means hunting down every copy.

The deciding question: how is it connected?

The choice really comes down to how your data is linked. If things are mostly self-contained trees that you read whole, the bundled document fits. If things are linked every which way, many items related to many other items in many directions, the linked tables fit far better, because that web of relationships is exactly what references and stored-once facts handle gracefully, and what bundling into documents handles badly.

A rough test: if you keep needing to combine records from different places to answer your questions, that is a sign the linked-tables shape suits you. If you almost always grab one whole record at a time, the document shape suits you.

In short

Linked tables store each fact once and connect data by reference, which avoids duplication and handles richly interconnected data well. Self-contained documents keep related data together, which makes reading a whole thing simple but duplicates shared facts. Match the shape to how your data is connected and how you read it: trees you read whole lean toward documents; tangled webs lean toward tables.

A medallion architecture moves data through three logical layers: bronze, silver, and gold. The names sound like rankings, but the useful idea is not that each copy is simply “better.” Each layer answers a different question:

  • Bronze: What exactly arrived?
  • Silver: What trustworthy facts can we establish?
  • Gold: What shape makes those facts useful for this consumer?

Data becomes progressively more validated, consistent, and purpose-specific as it moves forward. Thinking in terms of these contracts is more useful than thinking in terms of three buckets or schemas.

Bronze preserves the evidence

Bronze is the landing layer. It keeps source data as close as practical to the form in which it arrived, along with ingestion metadata such as source, arrival time, file name, batch identifier, or message offset. Its job is faithful capture, not correctness.

Suppose a source sends an order with a malformed timestamp, an unknown country code, or a field that silently changed type. Bronze should normally keep that record. Rejecting or “fixing” it during ingestion destroys evidence. Once the raw input is preserved, the team can improve its parsing rules and replay the data without asking the source to send history again.

This makes bronze an audit trail and a recovery point. Downstream tables are derived products; if their logic is wrong or their schema changes, they can be rebuilt from the captured input. Bronze is not necessarily the business system of record, but it is the pipeline's record of what the source delivered.

Silver establishes reusable facts

Silver turns source-shaped records into dependable, reusable data. This is where a pipeline typically parses types, validates required fields, standardizes units and timestamps, deduplicates events, resolves schema differences, and conforms shared identifiers. Invalid records can be quarantined with a reason instead of silently disappearing.

The important design question is grain: what does one row represent? A silver orders table might promise one row per order, while order_events promises one row per source event. Once that contract is explicit, analysts and downstream pipelines can reuse the data without each one inventing its own definition of duplicates, valid timestamps, or customer IDs.

Silver should usually remain detailed enough to support several unknown future uses. It is cleaned and integrated, but not yet compressed into one dashboard's answer. Data scientists and analysts may reasonably work directly from silver when they need trustworthy row-level detail.

Gold serves a particular use

Gold presents business-ready data for a known consumer or decision. It might be a dimensional model, a daily revenue aggregate, a customer feature table for machine learning, or a stable dataset behind an API. It applies business definitions and chooses a shape optimized for how the data will be read.

Gold is therefore not universally “the best data.” An aggregate that is perfect for an executive dashboard may be useless for investigating a single order. Gold is more useful for a specific purpose precisely because it may discard detail, join in context, precompute metrics, and rename technical fields in business language. Different consumers can have different gold products derived from the same silver facts.

One order moving through the layers

Layer Example representation Promise
Bronze The original JSON plus source and ingestion metadata We can show and replay what arrived
Silver A typed, deduplicated order with normalized currency and customer ID This row satisfies a documented data-quality contract
Gold Daily net revenue by region, or customer lifetime value This dataset answers a defined business need consistently

The transformations form a lineage: a gold number can be traced to silver facts and then to the raw records that produced them. When a definition changes, the team knows which derived layer to rebuild.

What the separation buys you

The layers separate concerns. Ingestion can focus on capturing unreliable source data without also implementing every business metric. Data-quality work can evolve independently of individual reports. Consumer models can change without reaching back into raw files and reimplementing cleanup rules.

They also give failures a boundary. A malformed record can land safely in bronze, fail silver validation, and enter quarantine while valid records continue. A broken gold transformation does not erase its inputs. Preserved inputs plus idempotent transformations make recovery a rebuild, rather than a forensic rescue.

Common ways to get it wrong

  • Using three names without three contracts. Copying the same data between folders adds storage and latency but no architectural value.
  • Cleaning too early. Destructive fixes during ingestion make new parsing logic impossible to replay against the original evidence.
  • Letting every consumer read bronze. Each consumer then recreates validation rules, and their answers drift apart.
  • Making silver report-specific. Silver stops being reusable when one dashboard's filters and aggregates are baked into its facts.
  • Building one universal gold table. Gold is a serving layer; different questions often deserve different models.
  • Hiding bad records. Dropping invalid data without counts, reasons, or quarantine turns data quality into silent data loss.

It is a pattern, not a law

A small pipeline does not automatically need three physical copies of every table. Bronze, silver, and gold are logical responsibilities; a system may implement them with schemas, tables, views, streams, or fewer materialized stages. If the data is already clean, the use case is narrow, and replay is easy, forcing all three layers can create more ceremony than safety.

Add a boundary when it creates a real contract: faithful capture, reusable trusted facts, or a consumer-ready product. The heart of medallion architecture is not the metal names. It is preserving the evidence, defining where data becomes trustworthy, and keeping business-specific answers derived and rebuildable.

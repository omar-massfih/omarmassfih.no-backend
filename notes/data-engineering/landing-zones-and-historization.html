A data platform has two needs that are easy to mix together. It needs a safe place for incoming data to arrive, and it needs a way to remember how that data changed. A landing zone solves the first problem. Historization solves the second.

The landing zone is the platform's doorway: an operational handoff between a source and the ingestion pipeline. Historization is its memory: a deliberate representation of past states so the platform can answer not only “what is true now?” but also “what was known or true then?” Neither term is just another name for bronze.

A landing zone is where ownership changes hands

External systems often deliver files rather than write governed tables. They might upload a CSV every night, place an encrypted archive in object storage, or stream messages into a queue. A landing zone gives those producers a narrow place to write and gives the data platform a controlled place to receive.

A landing zone commonly supports work such as:

  • checking that a delivery is complete and has the expected checksum;
  • scanning, decrypting, decompressing, or masking sensitive fields;
  • recording a delivery manifest and triggering ingestion;
  • quarantining corrupt files before they enter governed datasets;
  • isolating external write permissions from the rest of the data platform.

The landing zone is therefore usually operational and short-lived. A file may be removed or archived after the pipeline has safely committed it to bronze. Consumers should not query it, and downstream transformations should not depend on files remaining there forever.

Landing and bronze can be separate—or the same

Bronze is a durable, governed record of what the source delivered. Landing is the handoff point before that durable record exists. The distinction is useful when a source needs its own drop location, when files must be inspected before admission, or when landing and governed data need different access and retention policies: 

source
  → landing: delivery accepted, not yet trusted to be durable
  → bronze: delivery recorded durably and available for replay
  → silver: source records interpreted as trustworthy facts
  → gold: facts shaped for a consumer

But a separate physical landing layer is not mandatory. A managed connector or event stream may commit directly and atomically into bronze. In that design, bronze also acts as the landing point. Add a separate zone only when the handoff needs a real operational boundary; otherwise it becomes another copy with no distinct promise.

Historization asks which past you need

Keeping old files is useful, but it is not yet a complete history model. A useful history must say what changed, which entity changed, and when the change applies. That last question hides two different clocks:

  • Valid time: when the fact was true in the business world. A customer moved on Monday.
  • System time: when the platform received or recorded the fact. The source told us on Thursday.

If late and corrected data matters, retain both. Then the platform can answer two different questions: “what do we now believe the customer's address was on Tuesday?” and “what address did Thursday morning's report use?” This is bitemporal history. Not every system needs it, but every system should be clear about which clock its timestamps represent.

Three common ways to retain change

Periodic snapshots

Copy the full source state every hour or day. Snapshots are simple and make point-in-time reconstruction intuitive, but unchanged rows are repeated, changes between snapshots are invisible, and a missing row is ambiguous unless deletion semantics are defined.

Change data capture or an event log

Record each insert, update, and delete with ordering information. This is compact and precise, and it can reconstruct state at any captured point. The price is more careful processing: duplicates, out-of-order events, missed offsets, and tombstones all need explicit handling. Retention must cover the entire period from which the platform may need to rebuild.

Slowly changing dimensions

In a Type 1 dimension, an update overwrites the previous value. That is correction, not historization. In a Type 2 dimension, a change closes the current row and creates a new version: 

customer_id  city    valid_from  valid_to    is_current
42           Oslo    2025-01-01  2026-03-14  false
42           Bergen  2026-03-15  NULL        true

Type 2 makes business history easy to query and lets a fact join to the dimension version that was valid when the event occurred. It requires stable business keys, non-overlapping validity ranges, and a defined policy for late corrections.

History exists at more than one medallion layer

Bronze and silver preserve different kinds of history. Bronze normally keeps source history: every delivery or change as it arrived, with ingestion metadata and without silently rewriting the evidence. This supports replay, audit, and debugging.

Silver may build interpreted business history: deduplicated changes, stable entity keys, normalized timestamps, valid-from and valid-to ranges, and explicit deletes. This is where raw technical change becomes a reusable statement about how a customer, product, or account evolved.

Gold keeps only the history its product needs. A finance model might retain daily balances, while an operational dashboard exposes only current state. Gold should be rebuildable from the more detailed history behind it; it should not be the only place yesterday's truth survives.

The customer-address test

Imagine customer 42 changes address, the source sends the update twice, and a late correction arrives a week later. A robust design can explain all of these:

  • Landing proves which deliveries were accepted and whether each was processed.
  • Bronze retains both received messages, their source positions, and arrival times.
  • Silver recognizes the retry as a duplicate and models the corrected validity period.
  • Gold attributes historical orders using the agreed business rule.

If the design cannot explain a duplicate, a deletion, a late event, and a replay, “we keep history” is still only an aspiration.

What to decide explicitly

  1. Is landing temporary, and what proves that a delivery was safely ingested?
  2. Which data is immutable, and how long is it retained?
  3. Are changes captured as snapshots, events, CDC records, versioned rows, or a mix?
  4. Do timestamps mean source event time, business-valid time, or ingestion time?
  5. How are deletes, duplicates, out-of-order changes, and late corrections represented?
  6. From which layer can every derived table be rebuilt?

The clean mental model is: landing receives, bronze remembers what arrived, silver interprets how truth changed, and gold serves the history a particular use case needs. Keep these responsibilities distinct even when two of them share the same physical storage. A layer earns its place through a clear contract, not through its name.

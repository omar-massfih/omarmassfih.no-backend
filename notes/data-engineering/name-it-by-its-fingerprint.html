We usually name things by where they are, a file at a path, or by an arbitrary label we assign, record number 12345. There is a different and surprisingly powerful way: name a thing by a fingerprint computed from its own contents. You run the content through a function that boils it down to a short, essentially unique fingerprint, and that fingerprint becomes the name. This small shift has deep consequences.

Identical content, identical name

Because the name comes from the content, two copies of the exact same thing produce the exact same fingerprint, so they end up with the same name. That means the system recognizes them as the same thing automatically and can store it once instead of twice. Deduplication, not storing the same data over and over, falls out for free, without anyone having to hunt for duplicates.

The name verifies the content

Since the name was derived from the content, you can check that you received the right thing: re-compute the fingerprint of whatever you got, and see whether it matches the name you asked for. If someone hands you corrupted or wrong data, the fingerprints will not match, and you know instantly. The name is a built-in integrity check, so you never have to take on faith that you got what you requested.

Names that need no coordinator

The name also does not depend on where the thing is stored or who assigned it. Anyone, anywhere, computing the fingerprint of the same content arrives at the same name, with no central authority handing out ids and no risk of two parties choosing the same name for different things. The content names itself, consistently, everywhere.

The trade-off

There is a natural consequence to accept: because the name comes from the content, changing the content changes the name. You do not edit a thing in place and keep its name; a change produces a new fingerprint and therefore a new name. This pairs naturally with keeping old versions around rather than overwriting, and it means the name always refers to one exact, unchanging piece of content.

What to remember

Naming things by a fingerprint of their content, instead of by location or an assigned label, gives you three things at once: identical content is deduplicated automatically, the name lets you verify you got the right data, and names mean the same thing to everyone without coordination. A lot of power comes from simply letting the content name itself.

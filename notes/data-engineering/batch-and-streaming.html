People talk about "batch data" and "streaming data" as if they were different things. They mostly are not. Almost all data starts as a steady flow of events: a sale happens, a button is clicked, a sensor reports a reading. That flow never really stops. What we call a batch is just that flow with a line drawn across it.

Batches are slices of a stream

A batch is created by choosing a boundary. Take every event from the last hour, or every event until the file reaches a certain size, and treat that chunk as one unit. The boundary is something we add for convenience; the underlying events did not arrive in a neat group. A useful way to hold this is that data is unbounded until we decide to bound it.

This reframes the choice. The question is not "is this batch or streaming data?" It is "how often do we want to draw the line?" Draw it once a day and you have daily batches. Draw it constantly and you have something that feels like streaming. Even a streaming pipeline usually ends up drawing lines somewhere, because storage and reports downstream still like to work in chunks.

Waiting versus not waiting

There is a second choice that matters more than batch size: whether each stage waits for the one before it. In a waiting design, a stage cannot start until the previous stage has finished its whole batch. Everything is lined up in a chain.

That chain is fragile. If one stage fails, everything behind it is blocked, and you may have to rerun the whole thing. Because the stages are locked together, a problem anywhere becomes a problem everywhere. It is simple, but it breaks in an all-or-nothing way.

Why a buffer changes everything

The alternative is to let stages work independently and put a holding area between them: a place where finished work waits until the next stage is ready for it. Now each event can move forward on its own, without the whole batch marching in step.

That holding area does something important during a rush. When events arrive in a sudden burst, the buffer soaks up the flood so the next stage is not overwhelmed. The producer can run fast and the consumer can run at its own steady pace, and neither one drags the other down. A slow or briefly failing stage no longer stalls the entire pipeline; the work simply waits for it.

What to remember

Do not think of batch and streaming as different worlds. It is one flow of events, sliced more or less finely. The choices that actually shape a pipeline are how often you draw the boundary, and whether you lock the stages together or let a buffer sit between them. A buffer costs a little complexity and buys a lot of resilience, which is usually a trade worth making.

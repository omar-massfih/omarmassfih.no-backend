Not all decisions deserve the same amount of worry. Some choices are easy to take back if they turn out wrong. Others lock you in for years. A simple but powerful habit is to sort a decision into one of these two kinds before you spend energy on it.

Two doors

Picture a decision as a door. A two-way door opens both ways: if you walk through and do not like what you find, you can turn around and come back at little cost. Choosing a library you can swap later, or a setting you can change next week, is a two-way door.

A one-way door only opens forward. Once you go through, coming back is hard, expensive, or nearly impossible. Picking the core way your data is stored, committing to a platform everything else will depend on, or a choice that locks you to one vendor: these are one-way doors. The trouble is that people often treat both kinds the same, agonizing over trivial reversible choices and rushing the ones they cannot undo.

Match the caution to the door

For a two-way door, the fastest good decision is usually to just pick something reasonable and move on. If it is wrong, you will learn that quickly and change it cheaply. Spending days in meetings on a choice you could reverse in an afternoon is wasted effort, and the delay itself has a cost.

For a one-way door, slow down. This is where careful thought, wider input, and small experiments earn their keep, because the price of being wrong is high and lasting. The skill is not "always be careful" or "always be fast." It is telling the two kinds apart and spending your caution where it actually matters.

Prefer building more two-way doors

There is a further move: you can often turn a one-way door into a two-way door by how you design things. If parts of a system talk to each other through stable, agreed connection points, and hide their inner workings behind those points, then you can replace a part later without disturbing everything around it. A choice that would have been permanent becomes reversible.

This is why loose connections between parts are worth the effort. They are not just tidy; they keep your future options open. In a world where requirements and tools keep shifting, the ability to change your mind later is itself valuable, and good design manufactures more of it.

The point

Before deliberating, ask how hard this decision is to undo. Move quickly through two-way doors and save your real care for one-way doors. And wherever you can, design so that fewer doors are one-way, because staying able to change direction cheaply is one of the most useful properties a system can have.

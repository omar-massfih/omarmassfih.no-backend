--- title: Never Change It, Make a New One heading: Never Change It, Make a New One listTitle: Immutability as a Design Choice description: Why treating data as unchangeable once created, and making a new version instead of editing in place, buys you safe sharing, easy reasoning, simpler concurrency, and history for free. lang: en category: Programming date: 2027-08-18 dateText: Aug 18 tags: programming, immutability, concurrency, design, reasoning ---

There is a design choice that quietly prevents a whole class of bugs: make your data unchangeable once it is created. Instead of editing something in place, you create a new version with the change and leave the original untouched. It sounds wasteful, why make a copy when you could just edit? But the benefits are large enough that it is often the right default.

Safe to share

Immutable things are safe to hand out. If a value can never change, you can give it to anyone, anywhere, as many times as you like, and never worry that someone will alter it while you are depending on it. Much of the danger in shared data comes from one party changing what another is relying on. If nothing can change, that danger simply disappears.

Easy to reason about

When you look at an immutable value, you know what it is, permanently. You do not have to trace through the rest of the program wondering who might have modified it since you last looked. What you see is what it will always be. That certainty makes code dramatically easier to hold in your head, because a large source of "wait, how did this become that?" is gone.

Simpler when things run at once

Immutability also tames concurrency. Trouble between things happening at the same time comes from two parties touching the same data while at least one is changing it. If nothing ever changes, there is nothing to fight over: any number of readers can look at the same value at once, completely safely, with no coordination at all. A whole category of the hardest bugs cannot occur.

History for free

Because you create new versions instead of overwriting, the old versions are still around. That gives you a natural record of how things got to where they are, useful for understanding a bug, auditing what happened, or undoing a change. You did not have to build a history feature; it fell out of never destroying the past.

The cost, in perspective

The cost is real: you create more objects instead of editing one in place. But it is often smaller than it first appears. You skip a great deal of defensive copying and careful coordination that changeable shared data forces on you, and well-built systems share the unchanged parts between versions rather than duplicating everything. What looks like waste often nets out in your favor.

Worth remembering

Treating data as unchangeable, a new version instead of an edit in place, trades a little copying for safe sharing, easy reasoning, effortless concurrency, and a free history of changes. Those are exactly the things that get hard as programs grow, so reach for immutability more often than the instinct to just edit would suggest.

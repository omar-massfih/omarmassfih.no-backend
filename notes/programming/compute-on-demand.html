When you need a sequence of values, the obvious approach is to build the whole collection first and then use it. There is a different approach that is often much better: produce the values one at a time, only when each one is actually asked for. This is called being lazy, and it is more powerful than it sounds.

A tap, not a bucket

The usual way gives you a finished container holding every value at once, a full bucket. The lazy way gives you something more like a tap: each time you ask, it hands you the next value and remembers where it left off, ready to produce the one after when you ask again. It is a recipe that yields items on demand rather than a list sitting complete in memory.

Three things this buys you

First, memory. You never hold the whole sequence at once; you hold one item at a time as it flows past. That means you can process something far larger than would ever fit in memory, because only a single value is present at any moment.

Second, no wasted work. If you might stop partway, because you found what you were looking for or hit a limit, then you never computed the values past that point. Building the whole collection up front would have computed all of them, including the ones you never reached, for nothing.

Third, and most striking, you can work with sequences that never end. An endless stream of values is perfectly usable when you only ever pull as many as you need and then stop. You could not do that with a finished container, because building an infinite one never finishes.

The mental shift

The change in thinking is from "a container holding all the values" to "a source that produces the next value when asked." Once you see sequences that way, a lot of things click: this is exactly how data pipelines and streams work, with values flowing through one at a time rather than piling up into giant collections at each step.

The bottom line

When you do not need all the values at once, do not build them all at once. Produce them lazily, one at a time, only as they are asked for. You save memory by never holding the whole thing, you skip work on values you never reach, and you can even handle endless sequences. Think of a tap you draw from, not a bucket you fill.
